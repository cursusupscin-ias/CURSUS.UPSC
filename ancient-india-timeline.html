A. Period Overview

Period 1 traces human developmental stages in the Indian subcontinent from primitive stone-tool hunting-gathering to the first planned Bronze Age urban centers and their subsequent ruralization.

Chronological Range: Lower Palaeolithic (c. 2 Million BP / 500,000 BCE) → Middle Palaeolithic → Upper Palaeolithic → Mesolithic (c. 8000–4000 BCE / 12,000 BP) → Neolithic (c. 7000/6000–1000 BCE) → Chalcolithic (c. 2000–800 BCE) → Early Harappan (c. 2800–2600 BCE) → Mature Harappan (c. 2600–1900 BCE / 2500–1750 BCE) → Late Harappan (c. 1900–1300 BCE).
Geographical Context: Early human sites were strictly restricted to hilly river valleys and plateau regions (Soan Valley, Belan Valley, Narmada, Bhimbetka, Chotanagpur, South Indian granite hills) because thick, moist alluvial forest clearance was impossible without iron tools. The Harappan civilization flourished in the semi-arid, alluvial floodplains of the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra river systems (Sindh, Punjab, Cholistan, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Baluchistan, and Western UP).
Major Developments & Turning Points:
  • 1. Tool Technology Evolution: Core hand-axes and cleavers (Palaeolithic) → Flake scrapers → Blade-and-burin industries → Microliths (Mesolithic) → Polished stone axes (Neolithic) → Copper-stone tools (Chalcolithic) → Bronze tools and chert blades (Harappan).
  • 2. First Food Production: Environmental warming at the Ice Age transition (Holocene, c. 10,000 BP / 8000 BCE) led to plant cultivation and animal domestication (Mehrgarh, Belan Valley).
  • 3. First Urbanisation (Bronze Age): Technological breakthrough in grid town planning, standardized baked brick architecture, elaborate underground drainage, craft specialization, weights, undeciphered script, and long-distance trade with Mesopotamia (Meluha) and Oman (Magan).
  • 4. De-urbanisation: Climatic shifts, desiccating river systems, and administrative collapse led to the abandonment of urban centers and migration into rural Late Harappan agrarian successor cultures.

B. Detailed Chronological Timeline

1. Palaeolithic Age (Old Stone Age)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 2 Million BP (NCERT) / c. 500,000 BCE (RS Sharma) to c. 8000 BCE / 12,000 BP.
Event / Development: Human habitation across three distinct climatic and technological phases:
  • Lower Palaeolithic: Chopper-chopping and hand-axe/cleaver tool industries (Soan Valley, Belan Valley).
  • Middle Palaeolithic: Flake-based tool industries dominated by scrapers and borers (Soan, Narmada, Tungabhadra).
  • Upper Palaeolithic: Blade-and-burin industries; emergence of Homo sapiens and rock art shelters (Bhimbetka, Kurnool caves).
Explanation: Humans lived as nomadic, unorganized hunters and food-gatherers during the Pleistocene Ice Age, dependent on raw stone tools without knowledge of agriculture, pottery, or house construction.
Historical Significance: Represents the longest phase of human survival and adaptation to environmental and glacial cycles.
Causes and Consequences: Cold, dry glacial climate forced human camps to remain near natural rock shelters and perennial river valleys rich in wild game.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Tool typology (hand-axes vs flakes vs blades), site locations (Soan, Belan, Bhimbetka, Kurnool).
  • Mains: Environmental constraints on early human distribution and technological limits.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 4 (pp. 19–21); NCERT Theme 1 Timeline 1 (p. 25).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 4); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, p. 25).
2. Mesolithic Age (Late Stone Age / Intermediate Phase)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 8000 BCE to c. 4000 BCE / 12,000 BP to 10,000 BP.
Event / Development: Emergence of microlithic tool technology (tiny geometric stone blades) and transitional animal domestication.
Explanation: Onset of the warm, dry Holocene epoch caused fauna and flora shifts, enabling humans to expand into new ecological niches (Chotanagpur, Central India, South of river Krishna).
Historical Significance: Formed the crucial socio-ecological bridge between food gathering and sedentary food production.
Causes and Consequences: Post-glacial warming boosted vegetation and game populations, encouraging semi-sedentary seasonal foraging camps. Animal bones in the Belan Valley show early sheep/goat domestication around 25,000–8000 BCE.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Microlith tool characteristics and key regional sites (Bagor, Adamgarh, Langhnaj, Tinnevelly).
  • Mains: Impact of post-Pleistocene climate change on prehistoric human adaptation.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 4 (pp. 20–21); NCERT Timeline 1.
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 4); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, p. 25).
3. Neolithic Age (New Stone Age / First Food Production)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 7000/6000 BCE to c. 1000 BCE (subcontinental variations).
Event / Development: Adoption of polished stone celts, ground stone axes, plant cultivation (wheat, barley, rice, ragi), pottery (handmade and foot-wheel), and permanent village settlements.
Explanation: Three major regional clusters emerged:
  • Northern Group (Kashmir): Burzahom and Gufkral—pit dwellings, polished bone tools, coarse grey pottery, and burial of dogs with masters.
  • Southern Group (South of Godavari): Piklihal, Maski, Utnur, Hallur, Brahmagiri—granite hill settlements, cattle pastoralism, ash mounds, and ragi production.
  • Eastern/Central Group: Chirand (Bihar—bone tools in late Neolithic), Belan Valley/Allahabad (rice cultivation in 6th millennium BCE), Garo Hills (Meghalaya).
Historical Significance: Laid the agrarian foundation of the subcontinent; shifted human economy from food extraction to food creation.
Causes and Consequences: Polished edge-tools enabled clearing of light hill-forests; settled farming mandated pottery manufacture for grain/milk storage and cooking.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Specific site features—Burzahom pit-dwelling/dog burial, Chirand bone tools, Utnur/Piklihal ash mounds, Belan Valley early rice.
  • Mains: Regional variations in Neolithic subsistence and the origins of agriculture.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 4 (pp. 21–25); NCERT Timeline 1.
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 4); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, p. 25).
4. Chalcolithic Phase & Copper Hoard / OCP Cultures
Date / Approximate Range: c. 2000 BCE to c. 800 BCE (Ochre-Coloured Pottery / Copper Hoards: c. 2000–1800 BCE).
Event / Development: Emergence of stone-copper rural cultures using copper tools, microlithic stone blades, and painted Black-and-Red Ware (BRW).
Explanation: Regional village cultures developed outside the Indus plain:
  • Banas/Ahar Culture (Rajasthan): Ahar (copper smelting, absence of stone axes) and Gilund.
  • Malwa Culture (MP): Kayatha, Eran, Navdatoli (richest variety of cultivated foodgrains).
  • Jorwe Culture (Maharashtra): Inamgaon, Chandoli, Nevasa, Daimabad—fortified nucleated villages, 5-room mud houses, urn-burials under house floors.
  • Ganga-Yamuna Doab: Copper Hoards (harpoons, celts, anthropomorphic figures) associated with Ochre-Coloured Pottery (OCP).
Historical Significance: Founded the earliest non-Harappan agrarian villages in central and western India, introducing painted pottery and multi-crop agriculture.
Causes and Consequences: Local copper smelting (Khetri mines) expanded farming, but soft pure copper tools (lacking tin alloy/bronze) prevented urban growth and forest clearance in alluvial zones. Infant mortality was exceptionally high (numerous infant burials in Jorwe sites).
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Regional chalcolithic site mapping, Jorwe burial orientation (North-South in Maharashtra vs East-West in South India), Navdatoli crop finds, Copper Hoard artifacts.
  • Mains: Technological limitations of Chalcolithic cultures and why they failed to achieve urbanism.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 5 (pp. 26–32); NCERT Timeline 1.
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 5); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, p. 25).
5. Early Harappan Phase (Pre-Urban / Formative Phase)
Date / Approximate Range: Pre-2600 BCE (c. 2800 BCE – 2600 BCE).
Event / Development: Development of regional agro-pastoral communities with distinctive pottery, small settlements, and regional fortification (Amri-Nal, Kot Diji, Damb Sadaat, Siswal, Kalibangan pre-Harappan levels).
Explanation: Marked by regional diversity, lack of large public structures, and the first use of the bull/oxen for ploughing (evidenced by a pre-Harappan ploughed field with right-angle furrows at Kalibangan).
Historical Significance: Provided the indigenous cultural base from which the Mature Harappan urban civilization evolved.
Causes and Consequences: Expansion of flood-plain agriculture and local craft networks led to population growth, though localized burning and site abandonments occurred before the Mature phase transition.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Early Harappan sites (Kot Diji, Amri, Siswal), Kalibangan ploughed field.
  • Mains: Indigenous origin vs foreign diffusion theories of the Harappan civilization.
Textbook Study Pointers: NCERT Theme 1 (pp. 2–3); RS Sharma Ch. 6 (p. 43).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, p. 43); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, pp. 2–3).
6. Mature Harappan Civilization (First Urbanisation / Indus Valley Civilization)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 2600 BCE – 1900 BCE (NCERT) / c. 2500 BCE – 1750 BCE (Peak Mature: c. 2200–2000 BCE, RS Sharma).
Event / Development: Full-blown Bronze Age urban civilization across 1.3 million sq km in India, Pakistan, and Afghanistan.
Explanation: Defined by distinct features:
  • Urban Planning: Citadel and Lower Town layout, grid system streets intersecting at right angles, standardized burnt bricks (ratio 1:2:4), elaborate covered street drainage, Great Bath at Mohenjodaro, granaries.
  • Economy & Agriculture: Annual Indus silt farming (wheat, barley, sesamum, mustard, rice at Lothal/Rangpur, cotton/sindon), wooden ploughs, canal irrigation at Shortughai, water storage reservoirs at Dholavira.
  • Crafts & Trade: Bronze casting (dancing girl), steatite bead-making (Chanhudaro, Dholavira), shell cutting (Nageshwar, Balakot), cubical chert weights (binary/decimal system), undeciphered pictographic script (375–400 signs, written right-to-left), sea/land trade with Oman (Magan), Bahrain (Dilmun), and Mesopotamia (Meluha).
Historical Significance: Represents the earliest organized state and urban culture in South Asian history.
Causes and Consequences: Indus inundation agricultural surplus supported non-food-producing artisans, merchants, and administrators; central authority maintained rigid standards across widespread territories.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Specific site findings—Dholavira (water reservoir, signboard), Lothal (dockyard, rice husks, fire altars), Chanhudaro (bead factory, no citadel), Kalibangan (ploughed field, fire altars), Harappa (granaries, workmen barracks), Mohenjodaro (Great Bath, bronze dancer, priest-king, Pashupati seal).
  • Mains: Town planning, civic governance, trade networks, socio-economic structure, and the nature of Harappan state authority.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 6 (pp. 33–44); NCERT Theme 1 (pp. 1–21).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 6); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, Theme 1).
7. Late Harappan Phase (Post-Urban / De-urbanisation / Successor Cultures)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1900 BCE – 1300 BCE (NCERT) / after c. 1750 BCE (RS Sharma).
Event / Development: Abandonment of major cities (Mohenjodaro, Harappa, Cholistan sites); disappearance of script, seals, standardized weights, long-distance trade, and burnt brick structures; population migration into Gujarat, Haryana, and Western UP (Jhukar culture in Sindh, Cemetery H in Punjab, Late Siswal).
Explanation: Shift from a sophisticated urban state to localized, rural agrarian successor cultures.
Historical Significance: Marks the end of South Asia's first urban experiment; the subcontinent returned to a rural lifestyle for over a millennium until the Ganga Valley second urbanisation in c. 600 BCE.
Causes and Consequences: Multiple ecological and systemic factors—increasing soil salinity, desertification, river shifts/drying (Sarasvati/Ghaggar), floods, climate drying, and loss of trade networks led to the collapse of the central unifying state.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Late Harappan regional cultures (Jhukar, Cemetery H, Late Siswal), causes of decline.
  • Mains: Evaluate the causes of the Harappan decline and the nature of the transition from urban to rural life.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 6 (pp. 43–44); NCERT Theme 1 (pp. 17–18).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, pp. 43–44); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, pp. 17–18).

D. Historical Evidence Base

1. Archaeological Sites
Palaeolithic & Mesolithic: Soan River Valley (Pakistan), Belan Valley (Mirzapur, UP), Narmada Valley, Bhimbetka rock shelters (MP), Kurnool caves (AP), Bagor (Rajasthan), Langhnaj (Gujarat).
Neolithic: Burzahom and Gufkral (Kashmir), Chirand (Bihar), South Indian hill sites (Piklihal, Maski, Brahmagiri, Hallur, Utnur, Kodekal), Belan Valley (Koldihwa/Mahagara).
Chalcolithic: Ahar and Gilund (Banas Valley, Rajasthan), Kayatha, Eran, and Navdatoli (MP), Jorwe, Nevasa, Daimabad, Chandoli, and Inamgaon (Maharashtra).
Harappan Cities & Outposts: Harappa (Punjab), Mohenjodaro and Chanhudaro (Sindh), Kalibangan (Rajasthan), Lothal, Dholavira, Surkotada, and Nageshwar (Gujarat), Banwali and Rakhigarhi (Haryana), Sutkagendor (Makran Coast), Balakot (Pakistan coast), Shortughai (Afghanistan).
2. Inscriptions, Seals & Scripts
Harappan Seals: Over 2,000 steatite, square/rectangular seals featuring animal motifs (one-horned bull/unicorn, humped bull, tiger, elephant, Pasupati) and short undeciphered pictographic inscriptions (250–400 signs, written right-to-left).
Foreign Epigraphic Evidence: Mesopotamian cuneiform tablets (c. 2350 BCE onwards) record trade with Meluha (Indus region), Magan (Oman), and Dilmun (Bahrain).
Dholavira Signboard: A large 10-sign inscription carved in steatite found at Dholavira's gateway.
3. Coins
ABSENT IN PERIOD 1: No metallic currency existed in Period 1. Harappans and Chalcolithic communities relied entirely on barter and unminted commodity exchange. Standardized cubical chert weights (binary lower, decimal higher) regulated exchanges. Metallic coins (punch-marked silver/copper) appeared in India only in the 6th century BCE (Period 3).
4. Literary Texts
NO CONTEMPORARY INDIGENOUS LITERARY TEXTS EXIST FOR PERIOD 1: The Harappan script remains undeciphered.
Later Textual References / Retrospective Use:
  • Rigveda (compiled c. 1500–1000 BCE): Mentions pur (ramparts/forts) and Puramdara (Indra as fort-destroyer), cited by R.E.M. Wheeler to hypothesize Aryan destruction of Harappa.
  • Atharva Veda: Mentions charms, spells, and amulets against evil forces, reflecting non-Aryan folk beliefs similar to Harappan amulets.
  • Puranic References: Puranas mention primitive root-and-fruit gathering cave-dwellers.
5. Material Evidence Capabilities & Limitations

14

Capabilities: Radio-carbon (𝐶 ) dating fixes cultural sequences; pollen-analysis reconstructs past climate and vegetation (e.g., agriculture in Rajasthan/Kashmir c. 6000 BCE); animal bone analysis proves domestication; metal composition analysis identifies ore sources (Khetri copper).
Limitations:
  • 1. Vertical vs. Horizontal Excavation: Most sites are excavated vertically to establish chronological layers; costly horizontal excavations are rare, leaving complete city/village layouts unknown for many phases.
  • 2. Differential Preservation: Dry climates (Rajasthan, Gujarat) preserve artifacts well; moist, humid climates (Middle Ganga, Bengal) corrode iron/metals and destroy organic mud structures.
  • 3. Undeciphered Script: Harappan ideas, political organization, and literature cannot be verified without a bilingual decipherment key.
  • 4. Accidental Survival: Only broken/discarded artifacts or buried hoards survive; organic items (textiles, wood, leather) decompose in tropical soils.

F. UPSC CSE Relevance Section

1. Prelims Fact Register
Burzahom (Kashmir): Neolithic pit dwellings, polished bone tools, coarse grey pottery, domestic dog burial with human master in graves.
Chirand (Bihar): Late Neolithic site yielded significant bone implements at the confluence of Ganga, Son, Gandak, and Ghaghra.
Belan Valley (UP): Continuous stratigraphic sequence from Lower Palaeolithic → Middle Palaeolithic → Upper Palaeolithic → Mesolithic → Neolithic (rice cultivation 6th millennium BCE).
Utnur / Piklihal (AP & Karnataka): Neolithic cattle-herder sites featuring seasonal dung-encircled cowpens and burnt ash mounds.
Ahar & Gilund (Rajasthan): Banas Valley Chalcolithic sites; Ahar lacked stone blades/axes and specialized in copper smelting (Tambavati).
Navdatoli (MP): Narmada river bank Chalcolithic site; produced the widest variety of foodgrains in prehistoric India (wheat, rice, lentil, black gram, green gram, grass pea, ber, linseed).
Inamgaon (Maharashtra): Jorwe culture site; 5-room mud houses, circular pit houses, clay mother goddess figurines, adult burials in urns under floorboards.
Kalibangan (Rajasthan): Pre-Harappan ploughed field with intersecting furrows; Mature Harappan citadel/lower town, fire altars, wheel-made pottery.
Lothal (Gujarat): Tidal brick dockyard, rice husk remains, fire altars, terracotta horse figurine (doubtful), terracotta boat models, painted jar.
Dholavira (Rann of Kutch, Gujarat): Unique three-tier settlement layout (Citadel, Middle Town, Lower Town), stone masonry fortification, elaborate rain-water harvesting reservoirs, 10-sign signboard.
Shortughai (Afghanistan): Far-off mature Harappan trading outpost near lapis lazuli sources; contains direct evidence of canal irrigation.
Chanhudaro (Sindh): Tiny craft center (less than 7 hectares) exclusively devoted to bead-making, shell-cutting, seal-making, and weight-making; completely lacks a citadel.
Khetri Mines (Rajasthan): Supplied raw copper ore to both Harappans and Chalcolithic communities (Ganeshwar-Jodhpura culture).
Harappan Weights: Binary lower denominations (\(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 \dots 12,800\), where 16 was the base unit) and decimal higher denominations, made of cubical unblemished chert.
2. Mains Analytical Themes
1. Socio-Economic Impact of Environmental and Geographical Determinism: How rainfall gradients, river inundations, and local metal deposits governed the transition from Stone Age hill-settlements to Indus floodplain urbanism.
2. Technological Constraints on Urbanisation: Analyze why Chalcolithic cultures (Ahar, Jorwe, Malwa) remained rural despite possessing metallurgy, whereas Harappans achieved urbanism through bronze alloys and Indus silt agriculture.
3. Nature of Harappan Political Authority: Examine archaeological hypotheses regarding Harappan governance—merchant rule (Sharma), multiple city-states vs. single unified state (NCERT)—in the absence of palaces, royal tombs, and deciphered texts.
4. Trade Networks as Drivers of Bronze Age Urbanism: Evaluate internal and long-distance maritime/overland trade routes linking Harappa with Oman (Magan), Bahrain (Dilmun), Mesopotamia (Meluha), Khetri, and Badakshan.
5. De-urbanisation and Continuity: Assess whether the decline of Harappa represented total destruction or a localized socio-economic reorganization into rural Late Harappan agrarian cultures.

A. Period Overview

Period 2 covers the transition of early South Asian society from the semi-nomadic, pastoral tribalism of the Early Vedic phase to the settled, iron-aided agricultural society and territorial state formations of the Later Vedic phase, alongside the Megalithic Iron Age horizons of peninsular India.

Chronological Range: Early Vedic / Rig Vedic Phase (c. 1500 BCE – c. 1000 BCE) → Later Vedic Phase & Painted Grey Ware (PGW) / Iron Age Culture (c. 1000 BCE – c. 600 BCE) → Peninsular Megalithic Horizon (c. 1000 BCE – c. 1st Century BCE / CE).
Geographical Context: Early Vedic society was concentrated in the Saptasindhu region (eastern Afghanistan, Punjab, and the western margins of Haryana/UP). In the Later Vedic phase, human settlements moved eastward into the upper Ganga-Yamuna Doab (Kuru-Panchala region) and eventually expanded toward Kosala (eastern UP) and Videha (north Bihar). In peninsular India, Megalithic communities occupied the upland plateau regions of the Deccan, Andhra, and Tamil Nadu before descending into river valleys.
Major Developments & Turning Points:
  • 1. Advent of Indo-Aryans & Horse-Drawn Chariots: Introduction of horse-centered mobility, spoked wheels, and coat-of-mail armor (varman), leading to tribal hegemony over local populations (dasas and dasyus).
  • 2. Economy Shift (Pastoralism to Agriculture): Transition from cattle-rearing and pastoral transhumance (gavisthi) to sedentary plough agriculture in the Ganga-Yamuna Doab.
  • 3. Introduction of Iron Metallurgy: Appearance of iron (śyāma or krishna ayas) in Gandhara (c. 1000 BCE) and western UP (c. 800 BCE), facilitating clearance of light Doab forests and improved tool manufacture.
  • 4. Political Evolution (Tribe to Territory): Emergence of territorial identities (Rāṣṭra) from tribal units (jana → janapada), strengthening of royal autocracy via complex sacrifices (Rājasūya, Aśvamedha), and erosion of popular assemblies (Vidatha, Sabhā, Samiti).
  • 5. Varna Consolidation & Social Stratification: Transformation of open occupational orders into a rigid four-varna hierarchy (Brahmana, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Sudra), introduction of Gotra exogamy, and institutionalization of Upanayana (sacred thread) privileges.
  • 6. Peninsular Megalithic Culture: Development of iron-using grave builders who interred weapons, tools, and Black-and-Red Ware with the dead under massive stone circles or in clay urns.
  • 7. Intellectual Reaction (Upanishadic Thought): Philosophical revolt against priestly ritualism and excessive animal sacrifice, giving rise to Atman-Brahman inquiries around 600 BCE.

B. Detailed Chronological Timeline

1. Early Vedic / Rig Vedic Phase (Pastoral Tribalism)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1500 BCE to c. 1000 BCE.
Event / Development: Migration/advent of Indo-Aryan speaking clans into the Land of Seven Rivers (Saptasindhu); compilation of the early Mandalas (II to VII) of the Rig Veda.
Explanation: A predominantly pastoral, semi-nomadic society organized into patriarchal tribal units (jana, vis). Wealth was measured in cattle (gomat) and horses; wars were fought for cows (gavisthi).
Historical Significance: Represents the earliest foundational stratum of Vedic literature, religion, and social organization in India.
Causes and Consequences: Superior military technology—horse-drawn chariots, bronze dirks, and coats of mail—enabled Aryans to defeat indigenous groups (dasyus) and establish supremacy in Punjab. Voluntary tributes (bali) were offered to the rajan (chief).
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Rivers of the Rig Veda (Sindhu, Sarasvati, Parushni), tribal assemblies (Sabhā, Samiti, Vidatha, Gaṇa), Rig Vedic divinities (Indra, Agni, Varuna, Soma).
  • Mains: Nature of Early Vedic polity and economy; comparison between Harappan urbanism and Rig Vedic pastoralism.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 7 (pp. 45–50); NCERT Theme 2 (p. 28) & Theme 3 (p. 54–57).

2. The Battle of Ten Kings (Dāśarājña)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1400–1200 BCE (within the Early Vedic timeline).
Event / Development: Battle fought on the banks of the river Parushni (Ravi) between King Sudas of the Bharata clan (supported by Vasishtha) and a confederacy of ten kings (5 Aryan + 5 non-Aryan clans, supported by Visvamitra).
Explanation: Sudas emerged victorious, establishing the political supremacy of the Bharatas. Subsequently, the Bharatas merged with the Purus to form the Kuru clan.
Historical Significance: Led to the consolidation of the Kurus and Panchalas in the upper Ganga basin and provided the name Bharatavarsha for the subcontinent.
Causes and Consequences: Intra-tribal rivalries and disputes over priestley patronage (Vasishtha vs. Visvamitra) led to armed conflict and clan amalgamation.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: River Parushni identification (Ravi), participant clans (Bharatas, Purus → Kurus), priest roles.
  • Mains: Mechanisms of inter-tribal political consolidation in early Vedic India.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 7 (p. 46).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, p. 46).
3. Later Vedic Expansion & PGW-Iron Age Phase
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1000 BCE to c. 600 BCE.
Event / Development: Migration of Vedic clans into the Ganga-Yamuna Doab; compilation of the Sama, Yajur, and Atharva Veda Samhitas, Brahmanas, and early Upanishads; emergence of Painted Grey Ware (PGW) sites.
Explanation: Use of iron (śyāma or krishna ayas) enabled woodland clearance and sedentary agriculture. Rice (vrīhi) and wheat became primary crops alongside barley.
Historical Significance: Shift from pastoral semi-nomadism to a settled, territorial, agricultural society, setting the background for state formation.
Causes and Consequences: Iron tools and agricultural surplus allowed the growth of royal power, complex sacrifices (Rājasūya, Aśvamedha), and compulsory tax collection (Saṁgrahītrī).
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: PGW site geography (Hastinapur, Atranjikhera, Noh), Vedic terminology (vrīhi, śyāma ayas, saṁgrahītrī), 4 Varna ritual rights.
  • Mains: Role of iron technology and agricultural surplus in Later Vedic state formation.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 8 (pp. 51–59); NCERT Theme 2 (p. 28) & Theme 4 (p. 84–85).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 8) [107–137]; NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, pp. 28, 84–85) [452, 543–547].
4. Peninsular Megalithic Horizon
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1000 BCE to c. 1st Century BCE / CE.
Event / Development: Emergence of iron-using Megalithic grave builders in the upland regions of Deccan, Andhra, Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu.
Explanation: Characterized by burials enclosed by stone circles (cists, pits) or unencircled red pottery urns, containing iron weapons (arrowheads, daggers, tridents), agricultural tools (hoes, sickles), and Black-and-Red Ware.
Historical Significance: Forms the crucial material precursor to the historical Sangam Age kingdoms (Chola, Chera, Pandya) in South India.
Causes and Consequences: Upland pastoral-agrarian economy relied heavily on hunting and shifting cultivation before moving into fertile river deltas for wet paddy cultivation.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Megalithic burial types (cist, pit, urn burial), associated grave goods, Black-and-Red Ware, geographical spread.
  • Mains: Socio-economic organization of Megalithic communities and their transition into early Tamil statelets.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 17 (pp. 120–121); NCERT Theme 2 (p. 28).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 17) [265–267]; NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, p. 28).
5. Composition of early Upanishads & Anti-Ritualistic Philosophy
Date / Approximate Range: c. 600 BCE.
Event / Development: Philosophical reaction against priestly domination, elaborate ritualism, and cattle slaughter; compilation of the early Upanishads (e.g., Chhandogya, Brihadaranyaka) in Kuru-Panchala, Kosala, and Videha.
Explanation: Introduced doctrines of Atman (individual self), Brahma (universal soul), and Karma, questioning the efficacy of animal sacrifice.
Historical Significance: Provided the intellectual transition from ritualistic Brahmanism to heterodox ascetics (Buddhism, Jainism, Ajivikas).
Causes and Consequences: Destruction of cattle wealth in sacrifices conflicted with the needs of the emerging iron plough agricultural economy.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Philosophical concepts (Atman, Brahma), royal philosopher kings (Janaka of Videha, Pravahana Jaivali).
  • Mains: The Upanishadic revolt as an intellectual expression of socio-economic transformations in the 6th century BCE.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 8 (p. 58); NCERT Theme 4 (pp. 84–85).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, p. 58); NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, pp. 84–85) [545–547].

D. Historical Evidence Base

1. Archaeological Sites
Early Vedic / Bhagwanpura Horizon: Bhagwanpura (Haryana), along with three sites in Punjab; features 13-roomed mud structures containing both Late Harappan pottery and Painted Grey Ware (PGW) in a pre-iron context (c. 1600–1000 BCE).
Later Vedic / PGW Sites: Hastinapur (Meerut, UP), Atranjikhera (Etah, UP), Noh (Bharatpur, Rajasthan), Ahichchhatra (Bareilly, UP), Kurukshetra, Kausambi. Deposits show mud-brick/wattle-and-daub houses, iron arrowheads/spearheads, and rice remains (Hastinapur, 8th c. BCE).
Megalithic Sites: Brahmagiri, Maski, Hallur, Junapani, Nagarjunakonda, Adichanallur (Tamil Nadu), Sanur, and various sites across the Krishna-Godavari and Kaveri valleys.
2. Epigraphic & Numismatic Evidence
Mitanı & Kassite Inscriptions (West Asia): Kassite inscriptions of c. 1600 BCE and Mitanni inscriptions of 14th century BCE in Iraq/Syria record Aryan names and invoke Rig Vedic gods (Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Nasatya), proving the eastward movement of Indo-Europeans.
Coins: ABSENT IN PERIOD 2. Neither Early nor Later Vedic texts mention minted coins as actual metallic currency. Terms like Niṣka and Śatamāna in Vedic texts refer to prestige gold ornaments or measured bullion weights, not minted coins. Currency exchange operated through barter and cattle units.
3. Literary Texts
Rig Veda Samhita (c. 1500–1000 BCE): 10 Mandalas containing 1,028 hymns offered to naturalistic deities. Mandalas II to VII form the oldest core; Mandalas I and X are late additions.
Later Vedic Corpus (c. 1000–600 BCE):
  • Sama Veda: Rig Vedic hymns set to musical tunes for chanting during sacrifices.
  • Yajur Veda: Hymns accompanied by precise sacrificial procedures and ritual formulas.
  • Atharva Veda: Spells, charms, and prayers to ward off evil forces and diseases, reflecting popular folk beliefs and non-Aryan synthesis.
  • Brahmanas: Prose treatises attached to the Vedas explaining ritualistic details and varna obligations (e.g., Satapatha Brahmana, Aitareya Brahmana).
  • Upanishads: Philosophical texts focusing on Atman, Brahma, and spiritual knowledge over ritual sacrifice.
4. Material Evidence Capabilities & Limitations
Capabilities: PGW pottery stratification fixes Later Vedic cultural horizons; iron artifact analysis traces metallurgy from weapons (arrow-heads, spear-heads) to tools (axes, sickles); palaeo-botanical remains confirm crop shifts (wheat, barley, rice).
Limitations:
  • 1. Perishable Architecture: Later Vedic houses were constructed from mud, mud-bricks, or wattle-and-daub, which decay rapidly in moist Ganga plain soils.
  • 2. Lack of Contemporary Epigraphy: Textual traditions were preserved entirely through oral transmission (Śrutis); no contemporary indigenous inscriptions exist in India for Period 2.
  • 3. Poetic Hyperbole vs. Material Reality: Textual descriptions of magnificent cities (e.g., Hastinapur in the Mahabharata) do not match the modest mud-structure archaeological finds from 900–500 BCE layers.

A. Period Overview

Period 3 marks the political, economic, and socio-religious transformation of northern India. The focus of historical development shifted decisively from the upper Ganga-Yamuna Doab to the fertile, middle Gangetic basin (eastern UP and Bihar), giving rise to territorial states (Mahajanapadas), South Asia's second urbanisation, metallic currency, and major religious reform movements.

Chronological Range: Rise of 16 Mahajanapadas, NBPW & Punch-Marked Coins (c. 600 BCE) → Haryanka Dynasty of Magadha (c. 544–444 BCE) → Achaemenian Iranian Invasions (c. 516 BCE) → Sisunaga & Nanda Dynasties (c. 413–321 BCE) → Macedonian Invasion of Alexander (326–325 BCE).
Geographical Context: Core developments unfolded in the middle Gangetic plain (Kosala, Magadha, Anga, Vajjis, Mallas, Kasi, Vatsa) [106–112]. High annual rainfall (100 cm) and dense monsoon forests required heavy iron axes for clearance and iron ploughshares for alluvial paddy cultivation. The north-west (Gandhara, Kamboja) remained politically fragmented, exposing it to Iranian and Greek invasions. Central India saw the rise of Avanti around Malwa (Ujjain and Mahishamati).
Major Developments & Turning Points:
  • 1. Second Urbanisation & NBPW Phase: Growth of planned, fortified riverine and trade towns (Pataliputra, Rajgir, Kausambi, Sravasti, Varanasi, Vaishali, Champa) characterized by Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) and iron-tool craft specialization.
  • 2. Monetary Economy: Emergence of South Asia's earliest minted currency—silver and copper punch-marked coins (karshapana)—facilitating long-distance trade, merchant guilds (shrenis), and professional state taxation.
  • 3. Emergence of the First Magadhan Empire: Systemic expansion of Magadha over rival states through iron weapons, riverine communication, elephant warfare, and aggressive dynastic statecraft (Bimbisara, Ajatasatru, Mahapadma Nanda) [71–74, 114–126, 423].
  • 4. Heterodox Religious Movements: Socio-economic reaction against Brahmanical varna privilege and sacrificial animal slaughter, leading to the rise of 62 heterodox sects, most notably Jainism (Vardhamana Mahavira) and Buddhism (Gautama Buddha) [60, 72–80, 418, 521].
  • 5. Monarchy vs. Oligarchy (Ganas/Sanghas): Structural clash between centralized, tax-exacting hereditary monarchies (Magadha, Kosala) and clan-based, non-Brahmanical oligarchic republics (Vajji/Lichchhavis, Sakyas, Mallas).
  • 6. Foreign Contacts: Iranian Achaemenian conquest of Punjab/Sindh introducing Kharosthi script, followed by Alexander's campaign in 326–325 BCE, which weakened north-western tribal states and cleared the path for Mauryan unification [131–136].

D. Historical Evidence Base

1. Archaeological Sites & Ceramic Material
NBPW Ceramic Horizon: Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) serves as the diagnostic marker for Period 3. It was a very glossy, shining, fine-fabric tableware used by urban elites.
Key Urban Sites Excavated:
  • Rajgir (Girivraja, Bihar): Earliest Magadhan capital; enclosed by massive cyclopean stone walls built across 5 hills.
  • Pataliputra (Patna, Bihar): Capital built at the confluence of Ganga and Son by Udayin.
  • Kausambi (UP): Capital of Vatsa; fortified city with mud ramparts at the Ganga-Yamuna confluence.
  • Ujjain (MP): Capital of Avanti; major center for iron smelting, agate/carnelian bead manufacture, and fortification.
  • Sravasti, Varanasi (Rajghat), Vaishali (Basarh), Champa (Monghyr): All show clear NBPW urban habitation layers from c. 600 BCE.
Structural Remains: Burnt bricks and terracotta ringwells (used as soakage pits or domestic wells) appear in the middle NBPW phase (c. 3rd century BCE).
2. Epigraphic & Numismatic Evidence
Earliest Inscriptions (Prakrit in Brahmi/Kharosthi):
  • Achaemenian Inscriptions: Inscriptions of Darius I record Hi(n)du (Sindh/Punjab) as part of the Iranian Empire.
  • Early Votive Inscriptions: Short donor inscriptions on stone/images in cities recording gifts by weavers, scribes, potters, goldsmiths, and guilds (shrenis).
Punch-Marked Coins (Karshapana):
  • Earliest coins in India, dated to 6th–5th century BCE.
  • Made primarily of silver (extracted from Kharagpur hills, Monghyr) and copper.
  • Produced by punching distinct symbols (crescent-on-hill, tree, fish, bull, elephant) on cut pieces of metal; issued by royal state authorities as well as merchant guilds.
3. Literary Texts (The Primary Corpus)
Pali Canonical Texts (Buddhism):
  • Tipitaka ("Three Baskets"): Compiled after Buddha's death at Vaishali/Rajgir councils.
  • 1. Vinaya Pitaka: Monastic rules and regulations for the Sangha.
  • 2. Sutta Pitaka: Core discourses and dialogues of the Buddha (includes Digha Nikaya, Majjhima Nikaya, Sutta Nipata, and Therigatha—poems composed by nuns).
  • 3. Abhidhamma Pitaka: Philosophical and psychological exposition.
  • Jatakas: Over 550 folk tales detailing the Buddha's previous births; provide rich details of 5th–2nd century BCE social, economic, and merchant life.
Prakrit Canonical Texts (Jainism):
  • Written in Ardhamagadhi Prakrit; compiled finally at Valabhi (Gujarat) in 6th century CE. Include texts like Uttaradhyayana Sutta.
Sanskrit Sutra Literature & Dharmasutras:
  • Early Dharmasutras (Apastamba, Gautama, Baudhayana) composed from c. 500 BCE onwards by Brahmanas; codified varna duties, civil/criminal laws, and tax rules.
  • Ashtadhyayi of Panini (c. 500 BCE): Foundational Sanskrit grammar text giving key information on tribal janapadas and language usage.
Classical Greek Accounts:
  • Writings of Herodotus, Arrian, Nearchus, and Clitarchus detailing Alexander's campaign, Indian fighting qualities, sati, sale of girls, and Nanda military strength.
4. Material Evidence Capabilities & Limitations
Capabilities: NBPW stratigraphy provides precise chronological horizons for urbanisation; coin hoard analysis proves deep monetization of urban trade; archaeo-botanical remains establish paddy transplantation (vrihi).
Limitations:
  • 1. Moist Middle Ganga Soil Erosion: High humidity and acidic alluvial soils destroy organic items (wood, textiles, leather) and corrode early iron artifacts.
  • 2. Sectarian Bias in Texts: Buddhist/Jaina texts exaggerate Brahmanical corruption and Nanda greed; Brahmanical texts disparage non-Kshatriya rulers as "low born" or mlechchhas.
  • 3. Inability to Verify Poetic Numbers: Textual claims of 80,000 villages in Bimbisara's realm or 7,707 Lichchhavi rajas are conventional/symbolic figures rather than precise census data.

A. Period Overview Period 4 represents South Asia's first pan-Indian imperial experiment. Under the Maurya dynasty, the territorial expansion initiated by the Magadhan Mahajanapada expanded into an empire spanning from the Hindukush mountains in Afghanistan to the borders of the deep South. This era is marked by political integration, state control over economic activities, structured administrative bureaucracy, and the promulgation of Dhamma via royal epigraphs.

Chronological Range: Foundation of Mauryan Rule by Chandragupta Maurya (c. 321 BCE) → Treaty with Seleucus Nicator (c. 305 BCE) → Reign of Bindusara (c. 297–273 BCE) → Accession and Reign of Ashoka (c. 273/268–232 BCE) → Kalinga War (c. 261 BCE / 8th regnal year) → Later Mauryas & Disintegration (c. 232–185 BCE) → Overthrow of Brihadratha by Pushyamitra Sunga (185 BCE).
Geographical Context: Imperial core centered in the middle Gangetic plain at Pataliputra (modern Patna). The empire extended westward across the Indus basin to eastern Afghanistan (Kandahar, Kabul), Baluchistan, and Makran; northward to the Himalayan foothills (Kalsi, Rummindei); southward across the Deccan plateau into Andhra and Karnataka (Suvarnagiri, Maski, Brahmagiri); and eastward to Bengal and Orissa (Kalinga). The far southern tip (Cholas, Cheras, Pandyas, Satyaputras, Keralaputras) and parts of the extreme north-east remained outside direct Mauryan rule.
Major Developments & Turning Points:
  • 1. First Pan-Indian Unification: Chandragupta Maurya, assisted by Chanakya (Kautilya), overthrew the Nanda dynasty and repelled Seleucus Nicator, securing eastern Afghanistan and Baluchistan.
  • 2. Elaborate Bureaucratic State Control: Establishment of an administrative network managing taxation, justice, intelligence, and 27 specialized state departments (adhyakshas) controlling commerce, mines, liquor, and agriculture.
  • 3. Kalinga War & Ideological Transformation: Ashoka's conquest of Kalinga (c. 261 BCE) resulted in massive casualties (100,000 killed, 150,000 deported), driving Ashoka to renounce territorial conquest (bherighosha) in favor of ethical/cultural conquest (dhammaghosha).
  • 4. First Epigraphic Communication: Ashoka became the first Indian monarch to communicate directly with subjects through public rock and pillar inscriptions written in local vernaculars (Prakrit in Brahmi/Kharosthi; Greek and Aramaic in Afghanistan).
  • 5. Institutionalization of Dhamma & Welfare State: Appointment of Dhammamahamattas to enforce social harmony, tolerance, filial piety, humane treatment of slaves/servants, and animal life protection.
  • 6. Diffusion of Material Culture: Extension of Gangetic basin technologies—burnt bricks, ringwells, Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW), iron tools, and silver/copper punch-marked coins—to peripheral regions like Bengal, Orissa, Deccan, and South India.

D. Historical Evidence Base

HISTORICAL EVIDENCE MATRIX

1. Epigraphic Inscriptions:

• Asokan Edicts (44 Royal Order Sets): Engraved on natural rock faces (Major/Minor Rock Edicts), polished monolithic sandstone pillars (Pillar Edicts), and caves.

• Language & Scripts: Prakrit in Brahmi script (pan-India); Prakrit in Kharosthi script (NW - Shahbazgarhi, Mansehra); Aramaic & Greek scripts (Kandahar) [130, 425]. Deciphered by James Prinsep in 1838.

• Special Edicts: Rummindei Pillar Inscription (records tax reduction in Lumbini to 1/8th bhaga and exemption from bali); Kalinga Separate Rock Edicts at Dhauli/Jaugada ("All men are my children"); Kandahar Bilingual Inscription (Greek/Aramaic); Mahasthan Brahmi Inscription (Bogra, Bangladesh - records emergency grain storehouse).

2. Primary Literary Corpus:

• Arthashastra of Kautilya: 15 Books (Books II and III earlier core); details state monopolies, 27 adhyakshas, revenue assessment (*samaharta*), treasury (*sannidhata*), espionage, and civil/criminal law.

• Indika of Megasthenes: Preserved in fragments cited by Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus, Pliny; details Pataliputra city administration (6 committees of 5 members), army administration (6 subcommittees of 5 members), 7 social classes, absence of written laws and slavery (as understood in Greek terms).

• Buddhist & Jaina Literature: Ashokavadana, Dipavamsa, Mahavamsa, Hemachandra's Parisishtaparvan (records Chandragupta's adoption of Jainism).

3. Archaeological & Material Remains:

• Pataliputra Royal Remains: Kumrahar (Patna) excavations revealed traces of an 80-pillared stone assembly hall and wooden palisades/fortifications.

• Monolithic Polished Sandstone Pillars: Sarnath Lion Capital (National Emblem), Rampurva Bull Capital, Lauriya-Nandangarh Pillar; crafted from Chunar sandstone featuring mirror-like glassy polish.

• Rock-Cut Cave Architecture: Barabar Hills caves (Lomas Rishi, Sudama caves near Gaya) donated by Ashoka and Dasaratha to Ajivika ascetics.

• Currency & Infrastructure: Imperial silver/copper punch-marked coins bearing symbols of peacock, hill, and crescent; terracotta ringwells (soakage pits/domestic wells); burnt-brick structures across Ganga plain.

Material Evidence Capabilities & Limitations

Capabilities: Epigraphy provides firm chronological anchors (e.g., 8th regnal year for Kalinga War, 20th regnal year for Lumbini visit); spatial distribution of edicts maps the outer territorial boundaries of direct imperial administration; metallic analysis of silver punch-marked coins reveals state-standardized currency minting.
Limitations:
  • 1. Perspective Bias in Prashastis & Edicts: Royal edicts project the official imperial ideal (Dhamma) and ruler's perspective, which may not reflect actual administrative compliance in distant provinces.
  • 2. Fragmentary Nature of Foreign Accounts: Megasthenes' Indika survives only as secondary quotations in later Greek/Roman works, containing clear misunderstandings (e.g., claiming complete absence of slavery and absence of famine in India).
  • 3. Unpublished / Corroded Epigraphs: Physical damage to stone surfaces, missing letters, and faint engravings limit full textual translation.

1. Prelims Fact Register
Administrative Terminology (Arthashastra & Edicts):
  • Samaharta: Chief officer in charge of revenue assessment and collection.
  • Sannidhata: Chief custodian of the state treasury and storehouses/granaries.
  • Adhyakshas: 27 departmental heads regulating economic activities (mines, agriculture, trade, weights, liquor, weaving).
  • Rajukas: Provincial judicial and revenue officers appointed by Ashoka with power to reward and punish.
  • Dhammamahamattas: Special officers created in Ashoka's 13th regnal year to propagate Dhamma and prevent unjust persecution.
  • Pativedakas: Official reporters who had direct access to the king at all times and places.
  • Yuktas: Junior revenue/administrative officers assisting higher magistrates.
  • Vrajabhumi: Officer in charge of pastures and cattle pens.
City Administration of Pataliputra (Megasthenes):
  • Administered by a city council consisting of 30 members divided into 6 boards of 5 members each:
  • 1. Board I: Industrial arts and crafts regulation.
  • 2. Board II: Care and supervision of foreigners.
  • 3. Board III: Registration of births and deaths.
  • 4. Board IV: Regulation of trade, commerce, and weights/measures.
  • 5. Board V: Inspection of manufactured goods and quality control.
  • 6. Board VI: Collection of 10th sales tax on goods sold.
Military Administration (Megasthenes):
  • Managed by a board of 30 officers divided into 6 subcommittees of 5 members each:
  • 1. Infantry
  • 2. Cavalry
  • 3. War Elephants

  • 5. Navy
  • 6. Transport, Provisions & Logistics.

2. Mains Analytical Themes
1. Nature of the Mauryan State: Contrast the traditional model of a hyper-centralized bureaucratic autocracy (R.S. Sharma) with modern historiographical models emphasizing a metropolitan core (Pataliputra) controlling diverse, semi-autonomous peripheral regions (Romila Thapar / NCERT).
2. Ashoka's Policy of Dhamma: Evaluate whether Dhamma was an imperial religious propaganda tool for propagating Buddhism or an ethical civic framework designed to maintain peace across a multi-ethnic, multi-religious empire.
3. Decline of the Mauryan Empire: Critically assess the multi-causal explanations for the fall of the Mauryas—analyzing the interplay between financial depletion, administrative oppression in outer provinces, brahmanical reaction, loss of material monopoly, and frontier security neglect [163–167].
4. Spread of Material Culture: Explain how Mauryan imperial expansion acted as an engine of acculturation, diffusing Gangetic basin urban technologies (burnt bricks, ringwells, NBPW, coinage, script) to peninsular India and Bengal [143, 154–160].

A. Period Overview

Period 5 marks an era of intense political fragmentation accompanied by unprecedented economic expansion, international maritime and overland trade, urban flowering, and social/cultural assimilation. Following the disintegration of the Mauryan Empire, the Indian subcontinent witnessed two parallel political trajectories: Central Asian political incursions in the north-west and north, and regional state formations in the Deccan, Kalinga, and Tamilakam.

Chronological Range: Fall of the Mauryas & Rise of Sungas/Kanvas (c. 185 BCE – 28 BCE) → Indo-Greek Invasions (c. 200 BCE – 1st c. BCE) [187–190] → Cheti Dynasty in Kalinga (c. 1st c. BCE) → Saka and Parthian Invasions & Western Satraps (c. 1st c. BCE – 4th c. CE) [191–195] → Kushan Empire (c. 1st c. CE – 3rd c. CE) [196–200] → Satavahana Dynasty in the Deccan (c. 1st c. BCE – 220 CE) → Sangam Era Kingdoms in Tamilakam (c. 3rd c. BCE / 1st c. CE – 3rd c. CE).
Geographical Context:
  • 1. North-West & North India: Swallowed by waves of Central Asian groups (Indo-Greeks, Sakas, Parthians, Kushans) spanning from the Oxus River across Afghanistan and Punjab to Mathura and Varanasi.
  • 2. Central & Western India / Deccan: Sungas and Kanvas ruled Pataliputra and Malwa; Western Kshatrapas (Sakas) controlled Gujarat, Malwa, and Kathiawar; Satavahanas ruled the upper Godavari basin (Maharashtra) before expanding into Andhra and Karnataka.
  • 3. Eastern India: Cheti dynasty flourished in coastal Kalinga.
  • 4. Deep South (Tamilakam): South of the Krishna River, three early chiefdoms/kingdoms flourished—Cholas (Kaveri delta/Uraiyur/Puhar), Pandyas (Madurai/Tinnevelly), and Cheras (Kerala/Muziris) [234–237].
Major Developments & Turning Points:
  • 1. Central Asian Invasions & Social Assimilation: Indo-Greeks, Sakas, Parthians, and Kushans established kingdoms in northern India. Lacking their own script or established dogma, they rapidly adopted Indian religions (Buddhism, Shaivism, Vaishnavism), Prakrit/Sanskrit languages, and were integrated into the Varna order as "fallen Kshatriyas" (Vratya Kshatriyas).
  • 2. Silk Road & Indo-Roman Maritime Boom: Discovery of monsoon winds (c. 1st c. CE) accelerated direct oceanic trade between Egyptian-Roman ports and western/eastern Indian ports (Broach, Sopara, Arikamedu, Muziris). The Kushans controlled the Silk Road connecting China to Rome via Central Asia, extracting immense toll revenues.
  • 3. Monetary & Craft Revolution: Unprecedented circulation of metallic currency—gold dinars by Kushans, gold/silver Roman coins in peninsular India, and lead/potin/copper/bronze coins by Satavahanas, Nagas, and Yaudheyas [190, 197, 203, 216, 254, 255, 485–487]. Over 75 distinct craft occupations and organized merchant guilds (shrenis) are recorded.
  • 4. Rise of Mahayana Buddhism & Devotional Art: Emergence of Mahayana Buddhism (4th Buddhist Council under Kanishka in Kashmir) featuring image worship of the Buddha. Flowering of Gandhara, Mathura, and Amaravati/Nagarjunakonda schools of art [190, 208–209, 229, 230].
  • 5. Brahmanical Revival & Earliest Sanskrit Epigraphy: Performance of Vedic sacrifices (Aswamedha, Vajapeya) by Sungas and Satavahanas; issuance of the first long inscription in chaste classical Sanskrit by Saka King Rudradaman I at Junagadh (c. 150 CE).
  • 6. State Formation in the Deep South: Transition of Megalithic upland pastoralists to deltaic wet paddy cultivators, leading to the rise of early Sangam states and urban centers (Madurai, Puhar, Uraiyur) [232–237, 259].

1. Sunga & Kanva Rule in Central and Eastern India
Date / Approximate Range: c. 185 BCE – 28 BCE (Sungas: c. 185–73 BCE; Kanvas: c. 73–28 BCE).
Event / Development: Overthrow of the last Mauryan king Brihadratha by his Commander-in-Chief Pushyamitra Sunga; establishment of the Sunga and subsequent Kanva dynasties in Pataliputra and Central India (Vidisa).
Explanation: Marked an early revival of Brahmanical orthodoxy; Pushyamitra performed several Aswamedha sacrifices to commemorate victory over foreign invaders.
Historical Significance: Protected central India against deep Indo-Greek advances; patronized early Buddhist stone additions at Bharhut and Sanchi gateways.
Causes and Consequences: Sunga rulers consolidated Central India, providing a buffer against foreign invaders from the north-west.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Pushyamitra Sunga, Patanjali's Mahabhasya connection, Heliodorus Garuda pillar at Vidisa (under Sunga king Bhagabhadra), Bharhut stupa railings.
  • Mains: Evaluate the Sunga period as a phase of Brahmanical revival and art patronage.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 14 (p. 100) & Ch. 15 (p. 102); NCERT Theme 2 (p. 35).

2. Indo-Greek Invasions & Rule (Bactrian Greeks)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 200 BCE – 1st Century BCE (Peak under Menander: c. 165–145 BCE) [187–189].
Event / Development: Incursions of Bactrian Greeks across the Hindukush into Punjab, Rajasthan, and the Ganga-Yamuna Doab.
Explanation: Menander (Milinda) established his capital at Sakala (Sialkot) and engaged in a famous philosophical dialogue with Buddhist monk Nagasena (Nagarjuna), compiled in Milinda Panho (Questions of Milinda).
Historical Significance: First rulers in India to issue coins unequivocally attributed to specific kings; first to issue gold coins in India; introduced Hellenistic art elements that birthed Gandhara art.
Causes and Consequences: Pressure from Scythian nomadic movements in Central Asia forced Bactrian Greeks into India.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Menander (Milinda), Milinda Panho, Nagasena, Sakala capital, attribution of royal portraits on coins, introduction of gold coinage.
  • Mains: Contribution of Indo-Greeks to Indian numismatics and art.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 15 (pp. 102–104) [187–190]; NCERT Theme 2 (p. 44).

3. Rise of Cheti Dynasty in Kalinga under Kharavela
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1st Century BCE.
Event / Development: Expansion of the Cheti (Chedi) kingdom in Kalinga under King Kharavela.
Explanation: Kharavela led military campaigns against the Satavahanas, Magadha (retrieving the Jina image carried away by the Nandas), and Tamil chiefdoms. Generously patronized Jaina monks by constructing rock-cut caves on Udayagiri and Khandagiri hills near Bhubaneswar.
Historical Significance: Restored Kalinga's power following Asoka's conquest and promoted Jainism in eastern India.
Causes and Consequences: Agricultural expansion and iron technology in Kalinga provided the economic surplus for regional empire building.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Hathigumpha inscription (Pratishthana/Satavahana campaign, retrieval of Jina image), Udayagiri-Khandagiri Jaina cave architecture.
  • Mains: Historical significance of Kharavela's reign for Jainism and regional state formation in Orissa.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 9 (p. 63) & Ch. 21 (p. 148).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, pp. 63, 148).
4. Saka Rule & Western Satraps (Kshatrapas)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1st Century BCE – 4th Century CE (Vikrama Samvat Era: 58 BCE; Rudradaman I: c. 130–150 CE) [191–194, 216].
Event / Development: Ingress of Sakas (Scythians) who established 5 distinct seats of power: Afghanistan, Taxila, Mathura, Western India (Gujarat/Malwa), and Upper Deccan.
Explanation: King of Ujjain expelled Sakas in 58 BCE, founding the Vikrama Samvat era. In Western India, Saka Western Satraps ruled for 4 centuries. Rudradaman I defeated Satavahanas twice, restored Sudarsana Lake in Kathiawar, and issued the first major Sanskrit inscription.
Historical Significance: Promoted Sanskrit literature, repaired ancient public works, and facilitated western coastal trade.
Causes and Consequences: Displaced from Central Asia by Yuechi migrations, Sakas moved into north-western and western India.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Vikrama Samvat (58 BCE), Rudradaman I's Junagadh Rock Inscription (chaste Sanskrit, Sudarsana lake repair mentioning Chandragupta Maurya and Ashoka), Nahapana's silver coins restruck by Gautamiputra Satakarni at Nasik [193–195, 215, 210, 469].
  • Mains: Cultural assimilation of Sakas and their contribution to Sanskrit epigraphy and public works.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 15 (pp. 104–105) [191–195] & Ch. 16 (p. 114); NCERT Theme 2 (pp. 38, 41).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 15 & 16) [191–195, 215, 216]; NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, pp. 38, 41).
5. Parthian / Pahlava Rule
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1st Century CE (Gondophernes: c. 1st c. CE).
Event / Development: Rule of Parthians in north-western India on parallel lines with the Sakas.
Explanation: Occupied a smaller territory than Greeks and Sakas. King Gondophernes was the most famous ruler, during whose reign St. Thomas is traditionally said to have visited India to propagate Christianity.
Historical Significance: Facilitated early Christian contacts with South Asia.
Causes and Consequences: Westward push of Iranian groups into the Indus valley.
UPSC Relevance:

6. Kushan Empire & Kanishka
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1st Century CE – 3rd Century CE (Kadphises I & II: c. 50 CE onwards; Kanishka: c. 78 CE / Saka Era) [196–200, 497].
Event / Development: Expansion of the Yuechi/Kushan Empire from Central Asia (Oxus) across North-West India down to Varanasi; Kanishka's accession (c. 78 CE) marked the Saka Era.
Explanation: Kadphises I issued copper coins imitating Roman coins; Kadphises II issued extensive gold coinage. Kanishka established capitals at Purushapura (Peshawar) and Mathura, controlled the Silk Route, convened the 4th Buddhist Council in Kashmir (finalizing Mahayana doctrines), and patronized scholars (Asvaghosha, Charaka, Vasumitra).
Historical Significance: Integrated Central Asia and Northern India under a single administration, fueling international trade and Mahayana Buddhist art.
Causes and Consequences: Control over Silk Road transit tolls provided immense gold revenue, supporting gold coin minting.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Saka Era (78 CE), Purushapura (Peshawar) stupa, Mathura headless statue of Kanishka, 4th Buddhist Council in Kashmir, Buddhacharita by Asvaghosha, Charakasamhita, high-purity gold dinars, Devaputra title [198–199, 203, 206, 211, 466, 486].
  • Mains: Analyze the Kushan Empire as a bridge for trade, art, and religious transmission between Central Asia and India.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 15 (pp. 105–109) [196–200]; NCERT Theme 2 (pp. 36, 44) [465–466, 486] & Theme 4 (p. 104).
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 15) [196–200]; NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, pp. 36, 44, 104) [465–466, 486, 546].
7. Satavahana Empire in the Deccan
Date / Approximate Range: c. 1st Century BCE – 220 CE (Gautamiputra Satakarni: A.D. 106–130; Vasisthiputra Pulumayi: A.D. 130–154; Yajna Sri Satakarni: A.D. 165–194) [214–216].
Event / Development: Hegemony of the Satavahanas (Andhras) over Maharashtra, Andhra, and Karnataka, with capitals at Paithan (Pratisthan) and Amaravati.
Explanation: Gautamiputra Satakarni defeated Saka ruler Nahapana, restruck his silver coins, and claimed to be the "unique Brahmana" (Eka Bamhana). Yajna Sri Satakarni issued ship-motif coins reflecting maritime trade. Issued tax-free land grants to Brahmanas and Buddhist monks (Brahmadeya), created aharas (districts), and issued lead, potin, copper, and bronze currency.
Historical Significance: Stabilized the Deccan, introduced Northern material culture (coins, burnt bricks, ringwells) to South India, and built rock-cut caves (Karle, Nasik) and stupas (Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda).
Causes and Consequences: Agriculture in fertile Krishna-Godavari rice bowl combined with Roman maritime trade revenue generated imperial wealth.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Gautamiputra Satakarni (Eka Bamhana title, Jogalthambi hoard of restruck Nahapana coins), Matrilateral names (Gotamiputra, Vasisthiputra), Paithan capital, ship-type coins of Yajna Sri Satakarni, earliest epigraphic land grants, Prakrit official language (Gathasaptasati by Hala), lead/potin coins [214–216, 221, 225, 227, 231, 512, 517].
  • Mains: Examine the Satavahana administrative system, land grant policy, and role as a cultural bridge between Northern and Southern India.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 16 (pp. 114–119) [214–231]; NCERT Theme 2 (p. 43) & Theme 3 (pp. 59, 63) [512–513, 517].

8. Sangam Era in Tamilakam (Chola, Chera, Pandya Kingdoms)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 3rd Century BCE / 1st Century CE – 3rd Century CE [234–238, 241].
Event / Development: Emergence of three early kingdoms in the deep South: Pandyas (Madurai), Cholas (Uraiyur/Puhar), and Cheras (Muziris) [234–237].
Explanation: Pandyas ruled the southern tip (celebrated for pearls and early matriarchal traces noted by Megasthenes); Cholas ruled the Kaveri delta (famous for cotton trade and King Karikala who founded Puhar/Kaveripattanam); Cheras ruled Kerala (greatest king Senguttuvan - "Red Chera", famous for pepper trade and Roman trade at Muziris).
Historical Significance: Transition from Megalithic pastoralism to civilized statehood, literacy (Tamil-Brahmi script), and flourishing export trade with the Roman Empire.
Causes and Consequences: Discovery of monsoon winds accelerated pepper, muslin, pearl, and precious stone exports to Rome in exchange for Roman gold/silver coins [237, 238, 252–254].
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Sangam capitals and ports (Madurai, Uraiyur, Puhar/Kaveripattanam, Muziris/Cranganore), Karikala, Senguttuvan (Red Chera), Tamil-Brahmi cave inscriptions, Sangam literary corpora (Ettuthokai, Pattupattu, Silappadikaram, Manimekalai), Roman gold hoards, Yavanapriya (pepper) [234–238, 240, 241, 254].
  • Mains: Socio-economic organization of Sangam society and the impact of Roman trade on early South Indian urbanisation.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 17 (pp. 120–125) [232–241] & Ch. 18 (pp. 128–131) [252–254]; NCERT Theme 2 (pp. 35, 44) [462–464, 481, 484].

  • South India / Tamilakam: Arikamedu (Roman trading post, Arretine pottery, amphorae, dyeing vats), Uraiyur (textile dyeing vats), Kodumanal (bead-making industry), Karur, Kaveripattanam (Puhar).
2. Epigraphic Evidence
Junagadh Rock Inscription of Rudradaman I (c. 150 CE): First long inscription composed in chaste classical Sanskrit; records the repair of Sudarsana Lake (originally constructed under Chandragupta Maurya/Ashoka) without levying extra taxes.
Hathigumpha Cave Inscription of Kharavela (c. 1st c. BCE): Prakrit inscription in Brahmi script at Udayagiri hills near Bhubaneswar; details 13 years of Kharavela's military victories, canal repairs, and patronage to Jaina monks.
Nasik Cave Prasasti of Gautami Balasri (c. 2nd c. CE): Records the achievements of her son Gautamiputra Satakarni, describing him as Eka Bamhana (unique Brahmana) and destroyer of the Saka Kshaharata lineage of Nahapana.
Besnagar Garuda Pillar Inscription of Heliodorus (c. late 2nd c. BCE): Recorded in Prakrit at Vidisa; shows that Heliodorus, an ambassador of Indo-Greek King Antialcidas, converted to Bhagavatism (Vaishnavism).
Tamil-Brahmi Cave Inscriptions: Over 75 short inscriptions found in natural rock shelters around Madurai recording votive gifts by merchants and lay devotees to Jaina and Buddhist ascetics.
3. Numismatic Corpus ● Indo-Greek Coins: First coins in India with bilingual legends (Greek and Kharosthi) and accurate royal portraits; first gold coins. ● Kushan Coinage: Issued high-purity gold dinars (weight standardized to Roman gold coins) and abundant copper coins for daily urban transactions; depicted Indian, Greek, and Iranian divinities (Shiva, Buddha, Mithra). ● Satavahana Currency: Made predominantly of lead, potin, copper, and bronze; lacked gold coins; Yajna Sri Satakarni issued ship-motif coins symbolizing maritime trade. ● Indigenous Tribal Republic Coins: Yaudheyas (Punjab/Haryana) and Nagas (Central India) issued thousands of copper coins. ● Roman Coin Hoards: Over 85 hoards of Roman gold (aurei) and silver coins discovered across South India (e.g., Coimbatore, Madurai, Malayali hills), demonstrating the vast balance-of-trade surplus enjoyed by Indian exporters.
4. Literary Texts
Indigenous Literary Texts:
  • Milinda Panho: Pali Buddhist dialogue between Indo-Greek King Menander and monk Nagasena.
  • Mahavastu: Buddhist text cataloguing 36 craft occupations in Rajgir.
  • Gathasaptasati: Prakrit anthology of 700 erotic/pastoral verses attributed to Satavahana King Hala.
  • Buddhacharita & Saundarananda: Sanskrit kavya compositions by Kanishka's court poet Asvaghosha.
  • Sangam Literature: Earliest Tamil poetry corpus (Ettuthokai - 8 Anthologies, Pattupattu - 10 Idylls, Silappadikaram, Manimekalai) detailing social classes, war heroes, and Indo-Roman trade.
Foreign Accounts:
  • Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (c. 80–115 CE): Anonymous Greek sailor's guide detailing Indian ports (Broach, Sopara, Muziris, Arikamedu), trade routes, and export items (pepper, muslin, pearls, ivory).
  • Naturalis Historia by Pliny (c. 77 CE): Latin text lamenting the drain of 50 million sesterces of Roman gold annually to India for luxury goods like pepper (yavanapriya) and steel.
  • Geography by Ptolemy (c. 150 CE): Detailed trade geography and subcontinental ports.

A. Period Overview Period 6 represents the classical climax of ancient subcontinental imperial statecraft under the Guptas, followed by political decentralization, regional state formations, and the emergence of early medieval feudal structures across Northern and Peninsular India.

Chronological Range: Foundation of the Imperial Gupta Empire & Accession of Chandragupta I (c. 319–320 CE / Gupta Era) → Campaigns of Samudragupta (c. 335–380 CE) [258–263] → High Watermark under Chandragupta II Vikramaditya & Visit of Faxian (c. 380–412 CE / NCERT: c. 375–415 CE) [20, 264–266, 488, 508, 528] → Huna Invasions & Disintegration of the Guptas (c. 455–550 CE) [266–269] → Reign of Harshavardhana of Kanauj & Visit of Xuanzang (606–647 CE) [20, 310–321, 508] → Peninsular Hegemony: Chalukyas of Badami & Pallavas of Kanchi (c. 570–750 CE) [322–340] → Overthrow of Chalukyas by Rashtrakutas (757 CE).
Geographical Context:
  • 1. Northern Heartland: Shifted from Magadha/Pataliputra to Madhyadesa (Upper/Middle Ganga Doab, Prayag, Saketa, Kanauj) [254–256, 311–312].
  • 2. Eastern Expansion: Agrarian expansion into Orissa (Matharas, Nalas, Manas), Bengal (Pundravardhanabhukti, Samatata, Gauda), and Assam (Kamarupa) [294–309].
  • 3. Peninsula & Deep South: Deccan plateau dominated by the Vakatakas (Vidarbha) and Chalukyas of Badami (Vatapi); coastal Andhra under Ikshvakus and Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi; Tondaimandalam/Tamil Nadu ruled by the Pallavas of Kanchi; deep South under Pandyas of Madurai.
Major Developments & Turning Points:
  • 1. Imperial Integration & Classical Flowering: Reunification of Northern India under the Guptas through conquests, matrimonial alliances (Lichchhavis, Vakatakas), Sanskrit court literature (Kalidasa), classical cave architecture (Ajanta, Bagh), and scientific breakthroughs (Aryabhata, Varahamihira, Mehrauli Iron Pillar) [17, 264, 285–292, 476–477].
  • 2. Emergence of Land Grant Economy & Feudal Structures: Proliferation of tax-free land grants (Agraharas / Brahmadeyas) to Brahmanas, temples, and monasteries with administrative/fiscal immunities, transferring state sovereign rights to local donees and creating a intermediary landed class [14, 277–278, 298, 303, 331, 358–364, 487–491].
  • 3. Urban Decay & Monetary Shift: Closure of Silk Road trade and Roman trade bans led to the decline of E. Mediterranean demand for Indian silk/spices, resulting in the abandonment/ruin of early historic cities (Pataliputra, Kausambi, Sravasti, Vaishali) and a transition to localized, self-sufficient agrarian manor economies [11, 269, 279, 311, 365–366, 493].
  • 4. Shift of Political Center to Kanauj: Strategic shift from riverine commercial capitals (Pataliputra) to elevated, fortifiable agrarian-military Doab centers (Kanauj), making the Doab the primary seat of subcontinental power [311–312].
  • 5. Brahmanical State Formation in the Peninsula: Transformation of local tribal chiefdoms into Sanskritized monarchies (Chalukyas, Kadambas, Pallavas, Gangas) through royal Aswamedha sacrifices, genealogy creation by court priests, and temple construction (Pattadakal, Aihole, Mahabalipuram, Kanchi) [323, 326–332, 338–340].
  • 6. Social Crisis & Sub-National Crystallization: Puranic Kaliyuga social crisis (peasant tax resistance and Varna-samkara) [347, 358–359, 384], leading to the Kalabhra revolt in Tamilakam and the crystallization of regional linguistic/cultural identities (Gauda, Andhra, Karnataka, Tamilakam) [369–370].

B. Detailed Chronological Timeline

1. Foundation of Imperial Gupta Era & Accession of Chandragupta I
Date / Approximate Range: c. 319–320 CE.
Event / Development: Accession of Chandragupta I; inauguration of the Gupta Era (Gupta Samvat).
Explanation: Chandragupta I elevated the family status from local chiefs (Maharajas) to sovereign emperors (Maharajadhiraja) by marrying Lichchhavi princess Kumaradevi and establishing control over Magadha, Prayag, and Saketa [254–257].
Historical Significance: Re-established political pan-Indian integration in Northern India after a century of post-Kushan fragmentation.
Causes and Consequences: Alliance with the prestigiouse Kshatriya Lichchhavi clan provided social legitimacy to the Guptas (who were likely of Vaisya origin).
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Gupta Era start date (319–320 CE), Chandragupta I–Kumaradevi coin type, Maharajadhiraja title.
  • Mains: Socio-political factors enabling the rise of the Guptas in Madhyadesa.

2. Imperial Conquests of Samudragupta (Prayaga Prashasti)
Date / Approximate Range: c. 335–380 CE (NCERT: c. 335–375 CE) [258–263, 508].
Event / Development: Military campaigns of Samudragupta across 5 distinct geopolitical zones, recorded by court poet Harishena in the Prayaga Prashasti (Allahabad Pillar Inscription) [13, 258–263, 476–477].
Explanation: Adopted 5 distinct provincial policies:
  • Aryavarta (Doab): Violent extermination (prasabhoddharana) and direct territorial annexation.
  • Dakshinapatha (12 Southern Rulers): Capture and liberation (grahana-mokshanugraha), restoring thrones upon tribute payment.
  • Atavika Rajyas (Forest Kingdoms): Subjugation into servitude.
  • Frontier States (Nepal, Assam, Bengal, Tribal Republics): Tribute payment and personal attendance (pratyanta).
  • Foreign Rulers (Sakas, Kushans, Simhala): Self-surrender and diplomatic submission (atmanivedana) [262–263].
Historical Significance: Established imperial suzerainty across South Asia, earning him the modern epithet "Napoleon of India".
Causes and Consequences: Subjugation of tribal republics (Yaudheyas, Malavas) destroyed ancient republican buffer zones, leaving frontiers vulnerable to future invaders [258–262].
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Harishena, Prayaga Prashasti lines/language (chaste Sanskrit), 5 categories of conquest, Aswamedha coin type, Meghavarman's Gaya monastery request [13, 258–263, 476–477].
  • Mains: Compare the imperial consolidation policy of Samudragupta with the ethical policy of Ashoka.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 19 (pp. 135–137) [258–263]; NCERT Theme 2 (pp. 36–37) [476–477].
Source Attribution: RS Sharma (India’s Ancient Past, Ch. 19) [258–263]; NCERT (Themes in Indian History Part I, pp. 36–37) [476–477].
3. Reign of Chandragupta II Vikramaditya & Visit of Faxian
Date / Approximate Range: c. 380–412 CE (NCERT: c. 375–415 CE) [264–266, 508].
Event / Development: Conquered Western Sakas (Gujarat/Malwa); married daughter Prabhavati Gupta into the Vakataka royal house; made Ujjain second capital; visit of Chinese pilgrim Faxian (399–414 CE) [20, 264–266, 488, 528].
Explanation: Conquest of Gujarat gave the Guptas western sea-coast ports (Broach, Cambay), fueling trade prosperity. Prabhavati Gupta ruled as Vakataka regent, enabling indirect Gupta control over Central India.
Historical Significance: Represented the peak of Gupta political extent, economic wealth, and classical Sanskrit culture (Nine Gems / Navaratnas including Kalidasa).
Causes and Consequences: Faxian recorded a peaceful society, mild criminal law, absence of land revenue oppression, but glaring untouchability of Chandalas.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Saka conquest, Vikramaditya title, Prabhavati Gupta's Danguna copper plate inscription [264, 488–491], Mehrauli Iron Pillar ("Chandra"), Faxian's account of Chandalas, Ujjain second capital.

4. Huna Invasions & Disintegration of the Gupta Empire
Date / Approximate Range: c. 455–550 CE (Skandagupta: d. 467 CE; Yasodharman of Malwa: 532 CE) [266–269].
Event / Development: Invasions of Central Asian White Hunas (Toramana, Mihirakula); initial defence by Skandagupta followed by financial depletion and ultimate empire fragmentation [266–269].
Explanation: Huna horse-archers broke Gupta defences in the north-west. Rise of ambitious feudatories—Yasodharman of Malwa (set up victory pillars in 532 CE), Maukharis of Kanauj, Maitrakas of Valabhi, and Gaudas of Bengal—dissolved central authority.
Historical Significance: Terminated South Asia's classical imperial age; accelerated the transition to early medieval feudal regionalism.
Causes and Consequences: Loss of western trade revenues (Gujarat), debasement of late Gupta gold coins, and widespread conversion of administrative salaries into land grants.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Skandagupta's Junagadh inscription, Toramana/Mihirakula, Yasodharman's Mandasor pillars (532 CE), debasement of late Gupta dinars, Maitrakas of Valabhi [266–269].

7. Kalabhra Revolt & Social Transformation in Tamilakam
Date / Approximate Range: c. 6th Century CE.
Event / Development: Anti-landlord, anti-Brahmanical peasant uprising led by the Kalabhras across Tamilakam.
Explanation: Kalabhras ("evil rulers") overthrew traditional Chola, Chera, and Pandya kings, revoked tax-free Brahmadeya land rights granted to Brahmanas, and patronized Buddhist/Jaina monasteries.
Historical Significance: Represents an explicit class/caste revolt against the emerging early medieval feudal landed order in South India; suppressed only by a combined coalition of Pandyas, Pallavas, and Chalukyas.
Causes and Consequences: Followed by the rise of the emotional Bhakti movement led by Alvar (Vaishnava) and Nayanar (Shaiva) saints to integrate popular masses into the Puranic temple structure.
UPSC Relevance:
  • Prelims: Kalabhra revolt causes/nature, revocation of Brahmadeya grants, suppression by Simhavarman/Pandyas, early Alvar & Nayanar Bhakti saints.
  • Mains: Analyze the Kalabhra interregnum as a reflection of agrarian social crisis in 6th-century South India.
Textbook Study Pointers: RS Sharma Ch. 23 (pp. 159–160, 163).

D. Historical Evidence Base

HISTORICAL EVIDENCE MATRIX

1. Epigraphic Inscriptions:

• Prayaga Prashasti (Allahabad Pillar): Sanskrit kavya by Harishena on Asokan pillar; details Samudragupta's lineage, qualities, and 5-fold conquests [13, 258, 476–477].

• Mehrauli Iron Pillar Inscription (Delhi): Sanskrit record of King "Chandra" (Chandragupta II); famous for unrusting metallurgy over 15 centuries.

• Danguna Copper Plate Charter: Issued by Prabhavati Gupta (Vakataka regent); documents tax immunities granted to Acharya Chanalasvamin [488, 490–491].

• Mandasor Inscription (532 CE): Yasodharman's victory pillars over Hunas.

• Mandasor Silk Weavers Inscription (473 CE): Records migration of a silk weavers' guild from Lata (Gujarat) to Dashapura (Mandasor) and sun temple repair.

• Aihole Prashasti (Karnataka): Sanskrit poem by court poet Ravikirti glorifying Pulakesin II's defeat of Harsha on the Narmada and Kadambas of Banavasi.

2. Primary Literary Corpus:

• Harshacharita & Plays by Banabhatta: Biography of Harsha providing details of Vindhyan forest outskirts, royal army, and Kanauj court life.

• Classical Sanskrit Literature: Works of Kalidasa (*Abhijnanasakuntalam*,

*Raghuvamsa*, *Meghaduta*); Amarasimha's lexicon (*Amarakosa*); Bhasa's 13 plays; Visakhadatta's *Mudrarakshasa* & *Devichandraguptam*.

• Scientific & Technical Treatises: *Aryabhatiya* by Aryabhata (pioneered zero, decimal system, earth rotation); *Brihatsamhita* by Varahamihira;

*Charakasamhita* & *Susrutasamhita* medical texts.

• Foreign Pilgrim Accounts: Faxian's *Fo-Kuo-Ki* (399–414 CE); Xuanzang's *Si-Yu-Ki* (629–645 CE) [20, 313, 318–320]; Yijing (I-Tsing, 670 CE).

3. Archaeological & Architectural Remains:

• Classical Gupta Sculpture & Painting: Sarnath seated Buddha in Dharmachakra-Pravartana mudra; Sultanganj 2-meter bronze Buddha; Ajanta Cave Paintings (Caves 1, 2, 16, 17 - Vakataka/Gupta period).

• Early Structural Temples: Deogarh Dashavatara temple (Jhansi - earliest Sikhara) [287, 565–566]; Bhitargaon brick temple (Kanpur); Eran boar image.

• Peninsular Rock-Cut & Structural Temples: Mamallapuram Pancha Rathas & Shore Temple (Narasimhavarman I) [338–339]; Badami cave temples; Aihole Durga & Ladh Khan temples; Pattadakal Virupaksha temple; Ellora Kailasanatha monolithic rock-cut temple (commenced c. 8th century).

Material Evidence Capabilities & Limitations

Capabilities: Copper-plate land charters (Tamra-patras) preserve exact legal boundary markers, tax immunity lists, and regional administrative vocabulary; numismatic gold dinars enable precise metallurgical analysis of economic decline/debasement; physical temple monuments record architectural shifts from flat-roofed cells to Sikhara towers [287, 565–566].
Limitations:
  • 1. Eulogistic Prashasti Exaggeration: Court poetry (Prashastis) never records royal defeats or administrative failures (e.g., Harishena ignores Samudragupta's setbacks; Ravikirti glosses over Pulakesin's ultimate death at Vatapi) [13, 335–336].
  • 2. Monastic Bias of Chinese Pilgrims: Faxian and Xuanzang viewed India through a purely Buddhist lens, often reporting ancient Buddhist cities as thriving when archaeology shows structural decay, or exaggerating monastic student counts (e.g., Xuanzang's 10,000 monks at Nalanda vs. Yijing's realistic 3,000).
  • 3. Unexcavated Urban Layers: Overlying modern settlements above historic cities (Patna, Varanasi, Kanauj) prevent large-scale horizontal excavation.

F. UPSC CSE Relevance Section

1. Prelims Fact Register
Gupta Administrative Terminology:
  • Bhukti: Imperial province governed by an Uparika.
  • Vishaya: District unit governed by a Vishayapati.
  • Vithi: Sub-district unit in Eastern India.
  • Kumaramatya: High-ranking cadre of administrative officers serving as provincial governors, ministers, or military generals.
  • Sandhivigrahika: Minister for foreign affairs and peace/war (held by Harishena).
  • Mahadandanayaka: Chief judicial or military officer.
  • Gramakutumbina / Mahattara: Village elders/headmen managing local land transactions.
  • Vishti: Forced labour extracted from villagers for royal troops/officials.
Land Grant & Economic Terminology:
  • Agrahara / Brahmadeya: Tax-free land or village granted in perpetuity to Brahmanas or temples.
  • Devadana: Land granted to temples.
  • Nivi-dharma: Perpetual land endowment where donee could enjoy revenue but could not alienate the capital property.
  • Aprada-dharma: Land grant where donee had enjoyment rights but no right to grant it to others.
  • Dinar: Gold coin issued by Guptas, derived from Roman denarius.
Peninsular Administrative & Village Terms:
  • Ur: Traditional non-Brahmana village assembly of peasant castes in South India.
  • Sabha: Corporate assembly of Brahmana landholders in Brahmadeya / Agrahara villages.
  • Nagaram: Assembly of merchants in commercial/craft settlements.
  • Mahajana: Corporate council of village elders in Chalukyan Karnataka.
  • Gattika: Brahmanical educational institution attached to temples (e.g., at Kanchi) [328–329].
Cultural & Scientific Landmarks:
  • Aryabhata: Author of Aryabhatiya (Pataliputra, 499 CE); calculated π value to 3.1416, explained solar/lunar eclipses, stated earth rotates on axis.
  • Varahamihira: Author of Brihatsamhita & Panchasiddhantika (astronomy, astrology, flora/fauna).
  • Brahmagupta: Mathematician who formalized rules for operating with zero (Brahmasphutasiddhanta).
  • Navanitakam: Medical treatise on drugs and prescriptions of the Gupta age.
  • Sultanganj Buddha: 2-meter high copper/bronze standing Buddha statue.
2. Mains Analytical Themes
1. The "Golden Age" Debate: Critically evaluate the characterization of the Gupta period as the "Golden Age" of Indian history—balancing classical literary/artistic achievements against urban decay, untouchability, subjection of women, and forced labour (vishti) [269, 272, 279–281, 284].
2. Indian Feudalism Hypothesis: Examine R.S. Sharma's thesis on the origin of Indian feudalism through land grants (Agraharas), political decentralization, urban decline, peasant bondage, and self-sufficient village economies [269, 277–278, 358–368].
3. Geopolitical Shift from Pataliputra to Kanauj: Analyze the economic, strategic, and military reasons behind the decline of Pataliputra and the emergence of Kanauj as the premier seat of subcontinental power in the 6th–7th centuries CE [311–312, 493].
4. Evolution of Peninsular Temple Architecture: Trace the transition from rock-cut cave temples to monolithic structural stone temples under the Chalukyas of Badami (Aihole, Pattadakal) and Pallavas of Kanchi (Mahabalipuram, Kanchi) [323, 338–340, 565–567].
