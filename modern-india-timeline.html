2. Proposed Period Architecture (1498–1947)

To construct a logical, syllabus-aligned chronology, the uploaded materials are organized into seven distinct historical periods:

Period 1: European Arrival, Commercial Rivalry & Initial Footholds (1498–1757)

Chronological Span: 1498 (Vasco da Gama's arrival at Calicut) to 1757 (Battle of Plassey).
Core Themes: Portuguese Cartaz system and Blue Water Policy; Dutch spice trade focus and English entry under the 1600 Royal Charter; East India Company factory expansions (Surat 1613, Masulipatnam 1616, Madras 1639, Hooghly 1651); Farrukhsiyar’s Farman of 1717; Anglo-French Carnatic Wars (1740–1763).

Period 2: British Territorial Expansion, Colonial State Consolidation & Uprisings (1757–1856)

Chronological Span: 1757 (Plassey) to 1856 (Annexation of Awadh).
Core Themes: Dual Government in Bengal (1765); Mercantilist phase of colonialism (1757–1813); Policy of Ring Fence (1765–1813); Regulating Act 1773, Pitt's India Act 1784, Charter Acts (1813, 1833, 1853); Anglo-Mysore, Anglo-Maratha, and Anglo-Sikh Wars; Permanent Settlement (1793), Ryotwari & Mahalwari systems; Civil, Tribal, and Peasant Uprisings (Santhal Rebellion 1855–56).

Period 3: The Watershed Revolt of 1857 & Crown Reorganisation (1857–1884)

Chronological Span: 1857 (Sepoy Mutiny) to 1884 (Post-Ilbert Bill political consciousness).
Core Themes: Outbreak, spread, and suppression of the 1857 Revolt; Act for Better Government of India 1858; Indian Councils Act 1861 & Police Act 1861; Financial Decentralisation under Mayo (1870) and Lytton (1877); Deccan Riots (1875) & Deccan Riots Report (1878); Vernacular Press Act (1878); Ripon’s Local Self-Government Resolution (1882) and Ilbert Bill Controversy (1883).

Period 4: Socio-Religious Reform & Emergence of Organized Nationalism (1885–1914)

Chronological Span: 1885 (Formation of INC) to 1914 (Outbreak of World War I).
Core Themes: Social and Religious Reform Movements (Brahmo Samaj, Arya Samaj, Ramakrishna Mission, Aligarh Movement, SNDP); Moderates vs. Extremists; Partition of Bengal & Swadeshi Movement (1905–1908); Surat Split (1907); Morley-Minto Reforms & Separate Electorates (1909); Annulment of Bengal Partition and shift of capital to Delhi (1911).

Period 5: Mass Mobilisation, Gandhian Emergence & Revolutionary Nationalism (1915–1929)

Chronological Span: 1915 (Gandhi’s return to India) to 1929 (Lahore Session & Purna Swaraj).
Core Themes: Home Rule League Movement & Lucknow Pact (1916); Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad Mill Strike & Kheda Satyagraha (1918); Montford Reforms (1919) & Rowlatt Act; Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (1919); Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movements (1920–1922); Swarajist Party & Revolutionary Phase II (HRA/HSRA); Simon Commission (1927) & Nehru Report (1928); Declaration of Purna Swaraj at Lahore (1929).

Period 6: Civil Disobedience, Constitutional Experiments & Provincial Autonomy (1930–1939)

Chronological Span: 1930 (Dandi March) to September 1939 (Outbreak of World War II).
Core Themes: Salt Satyagraha & Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1934); Round Table Conferences (1930–1932); Communal Award & Poona Pact (1932); Government of India Act 1935; Provincial Elections of 1937 & Congress Ministries (1937–1939); Tripuri Crisis & Forward Bloc formation (1939).

Period 7: World War II, Negotiated Power Transfer, Partition & Independence (1939–1947)

Chronological Span: September 1939 (Resignation of Congress Ministries) to August 1947 (Independence & Partition).
Core Themes: Resignation of Congress Ministries (1939); Lahore Resolution for Pakistan (1940); August Offer (1940) & Individual Satyagraha; Cripps Mission (1942); Quit India Movement (1942); Indian National Army (INA) trials & Royal Indian Navy Revolt (1945–1946); Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) & Direct Action Day; Framing of the Constitution (1946–1949); Mountbatten Plan & Indian Independence Act 1947.

1. REFINED PERIOD OVERVIEW & KEY TURNING POINTS

Executive Overview

Period 1 (1498–1757) charts the transformation of European presence in India from sea-borne spice trading enclaves to armed corporate monopolies and territorial state-builders. Driven initially by the Portuguese goal of bypassing Ottoman-Arab Red Sea monopolies, the early 16th century introduced armed maritime control via the Cartaz permit system. By the 17th century, chartered joint-stock corporations from the Netherlands (VOC), England (EIC), Denmark, and France entered the Indian Ocean network.

While the Dutch eventually prioritized the Indonesian Spice Islands following the 1623 Amboyna Massacre, the English and French built fortified commercial hubs at Madras, Bombay, Pondicherry, and Calcutta. The disintegration of central Mughal authority after 1707 created a power vacuum in the Deccan, Carnatic, and Bengal. The Anglo-French Carnatic Wars proved that small, disciplined European-trained infantry sepoys could defeat larger traditional Indian armies. Leveraging internal political decay, private commercial privileges (dastaks), and covert conspiratorial diplomacy, the English East India Company defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah at Plassey in 1757, acquiring the 24 Parganas Zamindari and transforming a mercantile company into Bengal's political master.

Key Turning Points

  • 1.
    1498 (Vasco da Gama at Calicut): Opens direct European oceanic trade with Asia, bypassing overland Levant routes.
  • 2.
    1510 (Capture of Goa/Panaji by Albuquerque): Establishes the first sovereign European territorial enclave in India since Alexander the Great.
  • 3.
    1600 & 1602 (Royal Charters to English EIC & Dutch VOC): Institutionalizes corporate joint-stock capitalism armed with sovereign war and treaty-making powers.
  • 4.
    1612 (Battle of Swally) & 1613 (Surat Imperial Farman): Shatters Portuguese naval dominance off Gujarat and secures Jahangir's official sanction for the first permanent English factory at Surat.
  • 5.
    1639 (Madras) & 1690/1700 (Calcutta): Shifts English strategy from vulnerable factories to fortified, sovereign urban enclaves (Fort St. George and Fort William).
  • 6.
    1717 (Farrukhsiyar’s Imperial Farmans): Grants the EIC extensive duty-free trade and dastak privileges in Bengal, Gujarat, and Hyderabad—termed the "Magna Carta of the Company".
  • 7.
    1746 (Battle of St. Thome / Adyar River): French forces defeat the Nawab of Carnatic's army, revealing the structural tactical superiority of disciplined European infantry.
  • 8.
    1757 (Battle of Plassey): EIC overthrows Siraj-ud-Daulah through military conspiracy, acquiring the 24 Parganas Zamindari and establishing territorial dominance in Bengal (Note: The broader administrative Grant of Diwani occurred eight years later in 1765).

Pre-Study Orientation Tool for Period 2: British Territorial Expansion, Colonial State Consolidation and Uprisings (1757–1856), PERIOD OVERVIEW & KEY TURNING POINTS

17571856

Executive Overview

Period 2 (1757–1856) represents the century-long transformation of the English East India Company (EIC) from a commercial corporation in Bengal into the supreme sovereign paramount power over the entire Indian subcontinent.

Following the victory at Plassey (1757), the EIC defeated the combined forces of Bengal, Awadh, and Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II at the Battle of Buxar (1764), securing the formal Grant of Diwani in 1765 via the Treaty of Allahabad. Through Clive's Dual System of Government (1765–1772), the Company enjoyed untrammeled revenue collection while shifting administrative responsibility onto puppet Nawabs. As British Parliament intervened through landmark constitutional statutes—the Regulating Act of 1773, Pitt's India Act of

1784, and successive Charter Acts(1793, 1813, 1833, 1853)—the Company systematically

dismantled all major regional challengers:

Mysore was crippled through four Anglo-Mysore Wars (concluding with Tipu Sultan's death in 1799).
The Maratha Confederacy was shattered across three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818), dissolving the Peshwaship.
Sindh (1843) and the Sikh Empire in Punjab (1849) were annexed through forward military campaigns.

Concurrently, administrative and revenue mechanisms were deployed to extract agrarian surplus and enforce political dependence:

Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance System (1798) disarmed native states.
Cornwallis's Permanent Settlement (1793), Read & Munro's Ryotwari System (1820), and Mackenzie's Mahalwari System (1822/1833) reshaped rural agrarian relations.
Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse (1848–1856) and the annexation of Awadh (1856) on charges of "misgovernance" completed territorial consolidation.

This intense revenue extraction and administrative dislocation ignited widespread grassroots resistance, including civil rebellions, peasant revolts, and tribal insurrections—culminating in the massive Santhal Rebellion (Hul) of 1855–1856, on the eve of the 1857 Revolt.

Key Turning Points

  • 1.
    1764 (Battle of Buxar) & 1765 (Treaty of Allahabad / Grant of Diwani): Buxar establishes the EIC as a formidable military power in North India; the Treaty of Allahabad grants legal sovereign rights over the revenues of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.
  • 2.
    1773 (Regulating Act of 1773): Initiates British parliamentary oversight of EIC administration and centralizes executive power under the Governor-General of Bengal.
  • 3.
    1782 (Treaty of Salbai): Concludes the First Anglo-Maratha War, establishing twenty years of Anglo-Maratha peace and giving the EIC strategic breathing room to tackle Mysore.
  • 4.
    1793 (Permanent Settlement & Cornwallis Code): Fixes land revenue in Bengal permanently with Zamindars and enforces the institutional separation of revenue administration from civil justice.
  • 5.
    1799 (Fall of Seringapatam & Death of Tipu Sultan): Destroys Mysorean independence, establishing British dominance across Southern India.
  • 6.
    1802 (Treaty of Bassein) & 1818 (Dissolution of Maratha Confederacy): Peshwa Bajirao II accepts Subsidiary Alliance in 1802; by 1818, the Peshwaship is abolished and the Maratha confederacy dissolved.
  • 7.
    1813 & 1833 (Charter Acts of 1813 and 1833): 1813 ends the EIC monopoly over Indian trade (except tea and China trade); 1833 abolishes all commercial functions, redesignating the Governor-General of Bengal as the Governor-General of India.
  • 8.
    1849 (Annexation of Punjab) & 1856 (Annexation of Awadh & Santhal Rebellion): Completes territorial expansion to the North-West frontier and deposes Wajid Ali Shah in Awadh, coinciding with the Santhal Hul uprising.

1820
Event & Explanation: Ryotwari System formally introduced in Madras Presidency by Governor Sir Thomas Munro and Alexander Read (extended to Bombay Presidency under Elphinstone):

1833
Event & Explanation: Charter Act of 1833 passed by British Parliament:
1. Redesignates Governor-General of Bengal as Governor-General of India (Lord William Bentinck becomes the 1st Governor-General of India).
2. Abolishes all remaining EIC commercial privileges (including tea trade and trade with China); EIC becomes a purely political and administrative body.
3. Deprives Governments of Madras and Bombay of independent legislative powers; centralizes all legislative authority under the Governor-General in Council.

5. Lifts all restrictions on European immigration and land purchase in India.

1848–1856: LORD DALHOUSIE'S GOVERNOR-GENERALSHIP & DOCTRINE OF LAPSE
Doctrine of Lapse Policy:
  • Stated that an adopted son could inherit the foster father's private property, but not the state/gaddi; paramount power would decide whether to bestow the state or annex it by "lapse".
Sequential Annexations under Doctrine of Lapse:
  • 1848: Satara annexed.
  • 1849: Jaitpur (Bundelkhand) and Sambhalpur (Orissa) annexed.
  • 1850: Baghat (Himachal) annexed.
  • 1852: Udaipur (Central India) annexed. (Lower Burma/Pegu also annexed in 1852 after Second Anglo-Burmese War).
  • 1853: Jhansi annexed.
  • 1854: Nagpur annexed.
Major Non-Lapse Administrative/Infrastructure Developments:
  • 1853: ■ Charter Act of 1853: Recruitment to Civil Services thrown open to open competitive examination (ending Company patronage); 6 additional legislative members added (birth of Indian Legislative Council); Court of Directors reduced to 18. ■ First Railway line opened between Bombay and Thane (21 miles). ■ Electric Telegraph introduced (first line connecting Calcutta to Agra).
  • 1854: ■ Charles Wood's Educational Despatch ("Wood's Despatch"): Termed "Magna Carta of English Education in India"; outlines comprehensive educational hierarchy (primary vernacular schools, Anglo-vernacular high schools, affiliated universities). ■ Post Office Act of 1854 (uniform postage rates); separate Public Works Department (PWD) created in every province.
Historical Significance & Cause-and-Effect: Dalhousie's aggressive territorial usurpations and modern infrastructure create the centralized modern state while deeply alarming native ruling elites, setting the stage for 1857.
UPSC Relevance: BOTH | GS-I MAINS: Assessment of Dalhousie's tenure (Imperialist consolidator vs Modernizer); Prelims: Sequential order of Doctrine of Lapse states (Satara 1848 → Sambhalpur 1849 → Jhansi 1853 → Nagpur 1854), Wood's Despatch, Charter Act 1853, Railway/Telegraph 1853.
Study Pointer: Spectrum Chapter 5, 26, 30 & Appendices.
Source: Spectrum.
1855–1856: THE SANTHAL REBELLION(HUL)
Exact Dates: June 1855 – 1856.
Event & Explanation: Santhal Rebellion (Hul) breaks out across Damin-i-Koh (Bhagalpur to Rajmahal hills):
  • Led by four Murmu brothers (Sidhu, Kanhu, Chand, and Bhairav) and their sisters (Phulo and Jhano Murmu).
  • Santhals rise against heavy colonial land taxes, extortionate interest rates charged by diku moneylenders, and oppression by zamindars.
  • Proclaim an end to EIC rule and declare autonomous territory between Bhagalpur and Rajmahal.
  • Suppressed brutally by EIC troops by 1856; thousands killed, villages burned.
Administrative Outcome: Creation of Santhal Pargana (1856): Carving out 5,500 sq miles from Bhagalpur and Birbhum districts with special protective non-regulation laws.
Historical Significance & Cause-and-Effect: Largest armed anti-colonial tribal insurrection prior to 1857, forcing the colonial state to grant non-regulation agrarian status.
UPSC Relevance: BOTH | GS-I MAINS: Causes, nature, and outcomes of Santhal Hul; Prelims: Sidhu, Kanhu, Phulo, Jhano, creation of Santhal Pargana 1856.
Study Pointer: Spectrum Chapter 6 ("Santhal Rebellion") & NCERT Theme 10 ("The Santhals: Settled Pioneers").
Source: Spectrum & NCERT.
1856
Exact Date: February 1856.
Event & Explanation:
1. Annexation of Awadh: Lord Dalhousie annexes Awadh after deposing Nawab Wajid Ali Shah on grounds of "misgovernance". ■ Based on official reports submitted by Residents Sleeman and James Outram detailing administrative decay caused by 80 years of Subsidiary Alliance burdens. ■ Wajid Ali Shah exiled to Calcutta; widespread grief recorded ("The life was gone out of the body..."). ■ Summary Settlement of 1856 enacted in Awadh: Disarms taluqdars, destroys their forts, and strips them of land holdings.

1. PERIOD OVERVIEW & KEY TURNING POINTS

Executive Overview

Period 3 (1857–1884) represents a watershed structural transformation in modern Indian history. The simmering discontent generated by a century of East India Company expansion, aggressive land revenue settlements, economic ruin of traditional artisans, and religious-caste anxieties erupted in the Revolt of 1857. Beginning as a military mutiny at Meerut on May 10,

1857, it rapidly expanded into a popular anti-colonial war across Awadh, Rohilkhand, the

Gangetic Doab, Central India, and Bihar.

The suppression of the uprising by 1858–1859 marked the permanent end of Company rule and the formal extinction of the Mughal Empire. Through the Act for the Better Government of India (1858) and Queen Victoria’s Proclamation (Nov 1, 1858), direct administrative sovereignty was assumed by the British Crown, transforming the Governor-General into the Viceroy.

The post-1858 era initiated three major structural trends:

  • 1.
    Administrative & Legislative Institutionalization: The Indian Councils Act of 1861 reintroduced legislative devolution and non-official representation; the Indian Police Act (1861) and Indian Civil Service Act (1861) standardized the colonial coercive and bureaucratic apparatus.
  • 2.
    Shift in Colonial Agrarian & Social Strategy: Abandoning annexation and intrusive socio-religious reforms, the Crown cultivated princely states and zamindars as imperial "breakwaters". Agrarian distress shifted toward anti-planter and anti-moneylender popular movements, notably the Indigo Revolt (1859–1860) in Bengal and the Deccan Riots (1875) in Western India.
  • 3.
    Rise of Early Political Consciousness: Reactionary imperial measures under Lord Lytton (1876–1880)—including the lowered Civil Services age limit (1876), the Imperial Delhi Durbar during severe famine (1877), the Vernacular Press Act (1878), and the Arms Act (1878)—provoked organized political agitations. This culminated in the Ilbert Bill Controversy (1883–1884) under Lord Ripon, exposing white racial arrogance and uniting educated Indians to form all-India political organizations on the eve of the Indian National Congress.

Key Turning Points

  • 1.
    May 10, 1857 (Outbreak of 1857 Revolt at Meerut): Converts simmering sepoy grievances into a massive civilian-military war of independence.
  • 2.
    August 2, 1858 & November 1, 1858 (Act for Better Government of India & Queen's Proclamation): Abolishes East India Company rule; transfers sovereignty directly to the British Crown under a Secretary of State and Viceroy.
  • 3.
    1859–1860 (Indigo Revolt in Bengal): Peasant non-violent resistance against European planters, leading to the Indigo Commission (1860) and planter withdrawal.
  • 4.
    1861 (Legislative & Administrative Acts): Indian Councils Act 1861 introduces portfolio administration and non-official legislative members; Police Act 1861 and ICS Act 1861 codify provincial administration.
  • 5.
    1870 (Mayo’s Financial Decentralisation Resolution): Commences the institutional separation of provincial and local finances.
  • 6.
    1875 (Deccan Riots): Agrarian uprising against Marwari/Gujarati moneylenders in Poona and Ahmednagar, producing the Deccan Riots Report (1878) and Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act (1879).
  • 7.
    1878 (Lytton’s Repressive Code — Vernacular Press Act & Arms Act): Repressive measures designed to smother vernacular public opinion and disarm Indians.
  • 8.
    1882 & 1883 (Ripon’s Local Self-Government Resolution & Ilbert Bill Controversy): Ripon establishes democratic local bodies ("Father of Local Self-Government"); European opposition to the Ilbert Bill demonstrates the necessity of an all-India political organization.

SUB-PERIOD 3.1: THE GREAT REVOLT OF 1857 & SUPPRESSION (1857–1858)

1857(THE WATERSHED REVOLT)
Causes & Precursors:
  • Economic: Destructive land revenue systems (Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, Mahalwari), displacement of zamindars, loss of patronized arts/crafts, de-industrialization.
  • Political: Aggressive annexations via Doctrine of Lapse (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur) and deposition of Wajid Ali Shah in Awadh (1856).

  • Bareilly: Khan Bahadur Khan.
  • Faizabad: Maulvi Ahmadullah.
  • Baghpat (UP): Shah Mal (organized 84 villages - chaurasi desh; killed July 1857 by Dunlap).
  • Allahabad/Benares: Maulvi Liyakat Ali. Suppressed brutally by Colonel James Neill.

1. PERIOD OVERVIEW & KEY TURNING POINTS

Executive Overview

Period 4 (1885–1914) marks the emergence, institutionalization, and ideological maturation of organized Indian nationalism. The foundation of the Indian National Congress (INC) in December 1885 at Bombay provided a permanent all-India platform for politically conscious intelligentsia.

During its initial Moderate Phase (1885–1905), early leaders like Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Dinshaw Wacha, W.C. Bonnerjee, and Gopal Krishna Gokhale pursued constitutional agitation through petitions, prayers, and public education. Their enduring historic contribution was the Economic Critique of British Imperialism (the "Drain of Wealth" theory), which exposed the colonial exploitation behind British claims of a "providential mission".

The turn of the 20th century witnessed the rise of Militant Nationalism (Extremism) led by the "Lal-Bal-Pal" trio (Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, and Bipin Chandra Pal) and Aurobindo Ghosh. Driven by frustration over the limited gains of Moderate constitutionalism, Lord Curzon’s reactionary measures, and international events crushing the myth of European invincibility, the movement erupted into the Swadeshi and Boycott Movement (1905–1908) following the Partition of Bengal (1905).

The resulting tactical rift over extending boycott methods across India led to the disastrous Surat Split of 1907. Exploiting the split, the British government executed a strategy of repression and conciliation ("carrot and stick"): suppressing Extremists through harsh press laws and trials, while placating Moderates and propping up the All-India Muslim League (1906) through the Morley-Minto Reforms / Indian Councils Act of 1909. Concurrently, the closing of constitutional avenues spurred the First Phase of Revolutionary Activities (1907–1917) across Bengal, Maharashtra, Punjab, and abroad (India House, Ghadar Party).

Key Turning Points

  • 1.
    December 28–31, 1885 (Foundation of the Indian National Congress): Unites regional political associations into a single national organization.
  • 2.
    1892 (Indian Councils Act 1892): Introduces the principle of representation and limited financial discussion in legislative councils in response to INC demands.
  • 3.
    1897 (Assassination of Rand & Tilak’s Trial): Chapekar brothers assassinate Plague Commissioner Rand; Tilak’s 18-month imprisonment transforms him into an all-India hero of militant nationalism.
  • 4.
    July & October 16, 1905 (Partition of Bengal & Swadeshi Proclamation): Curzon's partition triggers the Swadeshi and Boycott Movement, introducing mass politics, passive resistance, and cultural revivalism.
  • 5.
    October & December 1906 (Shimla Deputation & Formation of Muslim League): Agha Khan leads the Shimla Deputation demanding separate electorates; Muslim League founded at Dacca as an anti-Congress elite front.
  • 6.
    December 1907 (Surat Split): INC splits into Moderate and Extremist factions at Surat over leadership and boycott methods.
  • 7.
    1909 (Morley-Minto Reforms / Indian Councils Act 1909): Introduces separate electorates for Muslims, institutionalizing communal representation in Indian legislatures.
  • 8.
    December 1911 (Delhi Durbar & Annulment of Partition): Partition of Bengal annulled; imperial capital shifted from Calcutta to Delhi.
  • 9.
    1913 (Foundation of the Ghadar Party): Indian emigrants in North America establish an international armed revolutionary organization.

SUB-PERIOD 4.2: SWADESHI MOVEMENT, SURAT SPLIT & MORLEY-MINTO REFORMS (1905–1909)

1905
Exact Chronological Sequence:
  • July 1905: Formal government announcement of the Partition of Bengal.
  • August 7, 1905: Boycott Resolution passed at a massive meeting in Calcutta Townhall; formal proclamation of the Swadeshi Movement.
  • October 16, 1905: Partition of Bengal comes into effect. Observed as a Day of Mourning throughout Bengal; people fast, bathe in the Ganga, walk barefoot singing Vande Mataram, and tie rakhis (proposed by Rabindranath Tagore and Ramendrasundar Trivedi). Tagore composes Amar Sonar Bangla.
  • December 1905: INC Benares Session: Presided over by Gopal Krishna Gokhale. Condemns Bengal partition and Curzon's policies; supports Swadeshi and Boycott movement within Bengal.
Key Organizational/Institutional Developments in 1905:
  • Servants of India Society founded by G.K. Gokhale with M.G. Ranade's guidance.
  • Indian Home Rule Society ("India House") founded in London by Shyamji Krishnavarma; brings out the journal The Indian Sociologist.
  • National Council of Education setup initiated by Satish Chandra Mukherji's Dawn Society.
Historical Significance & Cause-and-Effect: Converts a regional administrative protest into an all-India mass struggle, introducing new political techniques (boycott, passive resistance, national education, samitis).
UPSC Relevance: BOTH | GS-I MAINS: Methods, extent of mass participation, and impact of Swadeshi Movement; Prelims: Aug 7 & Oct 16 dates, Benares session 1905, India House London.
Study Pointer: Spectrum Chapter 12 ("Swadeshi Movement") & NCERT Theme 12.
Source: Spectrum & NCERT.
1906
Exact Chronological Sequence:
  • April 1906: Police brutalities at Barisal Conference; Yugantar weekly started by Barindra Kumar Ghosh and Bhupendranath Dutta.
  • August 15, 1906: National Council of Education formally set up; Bengal National College established with Aurobindo Ghosh as Principal.
  • October 1, 1906: Shimla Deputation: A group of Muslim elites led by the Agha Khan meets Viceroy Lord Minto to demand separate electorates and representation in excess of numerical strength.
  • December 30, 1906: All-India Muslim League founded at Dacca by Nawab Salimullah, Mohsin-ul-Mulk, and Waqar-ul-Mulk.
  • December 1906: INC Calcutta Session: Presided over by Dadabhai Naoroji. Passes four historic resolutions on Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education, and Swaraj (self-government like UK/colonies declared as goal for the first time).
Other Major Swadeshi Ventures in 1906:
  • Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company established at Tuticorin by V.O. Chidambaram Pillai.
  • Swadesh Bandhab Samiti formed in Barisal by Ashwini Kumar Dutt.
  • Bengal Chemicals Factory expanded by Acharya P.C. Ray.
Historical Significance & Cause-and-Effect: High-water mark of Swadeshi radicalism; simultaneously sees the institutional birth of Muslim communal politics supported by British authorities.
UPSC Relevance: BOTH | GS-I MAINS: Emergence of Muslim League; 1906 INC resolutions; Prelims: Shimla Deputation date, Swadeshi Steam Navigation Co, National Council of Education.

Exact Date: December 1907 (Surat Session).
Event & Explanation:

The Surat Split: INC splits at the Surat session (presided over by Rashbehari Ghosh). ■ Cause: Extremists wanted the session in Nagpur, Tilak or Lajpat Rai as President, and extension of Swadeshi/Boycott nationwide. 1. PERIOD OVERVIEW & KEY TURNING POINTS

Executive Overview

Period 6 (1935–1947) captures the final, explosive decade of British colonial rule in India, marked by the dual dynamics of an intense anti-imperialist struggle and the tragic march toward Partition.

The period opened with the implementation of the Government of India Act, 1935, leading to the 1937 Provincial Elections where Congress formed ministries in 8 out of 11 provinces. The unilateral entry of India into World War II in September 1939 without consulting Indian representatives prompted the resignation of Congress ministries in October 1939, celebrated by the Muslim League as the "Day of Deliverance".

As wartime pressures mounted, constitutional deadlocks deepened despite the August Offer (1940) and Stafford Cripps' Cripps Mission (1942). In response, Mahatma Gandhi launched his final all-India mass struggle—the Quit India Movement (August 1942)—which, despite immediate arrests of top leaders, erupted into widespread underground resistance and parallel governments in Ballia, Tamluk, and Satara. Simultaneously, Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose reorganized the Indian National Army (INA / Azad Hind Fauj) abroad, establishing a provisional government and marching toward the North-East frontier.

Post-war conditions made British withdrawal inevitable. The INA Trials at Red Fort (1945) and the Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (Feb 1946) triggered overwhelming anti-imperialist sentiment. However, the failure of the Wavell Plan (1945) and the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) to reconcile the Congress vision of a united India with Jinnah’s unyielding demand for Pakistan led to the Muslim League’s "Direct Action Day" (August 16, 1946) and catastrophic communal riots.

Following Clement Attlee’s Statement (Feb 1947), Lord Mountbatten arrived as Viceroy and enacted the Mountbatten Plan (June 3, 1947). The subsequent Indian Independence Act,

1947 terminated British rule, birthing two independent dominions—Pakistan on August 14,
1947 and India on August 15, 1947.

Key Turning Points

  • 1.
    February 1937 (1937 Provincial Elections): Congress wins sweeping majorities in general constituencies, forming 28-month ministries in 8 provinces.
  • 2.
    September 3 & October 23, 1939 (Outbreak of WWII & Resignation of Congress Ministries): Unilateral British declaration of war leads to Congress ministry resignations and constitutional deadlock.
  • 3.
    March 23–24, 1940 (Lahore 'Pakistan' Resolution): The Muslim League formally demands autonomous, sovereign statehood for Muslim-majority zones in the North-West and East.
  • 4.
    March 1942 (Cripps Mission Failure): Cripps' offer of post-war Dominion Status and provincial secession options is rejected, clearing the path for mass revolt.
  • 5.
    August 8–9, 1942 (Quit India Resolution & Operation Zero Hour): AICC adopts 'Quit India' at Gowalia Tank; Gandhi gives the "Do or Die" slogan; top leaders arrested.
  • 6.
    October 21, 1943 (Provisional Government of Azad Hind Formed): Subhas Chandra Bose assumes leadership of the INA at Singapore.
  • 7.
    November 1945 & February 18, 1946 (Red Fort INA Trials & RIN Mutiny): Widespread public outrage over INA trials and naval ratings' revolt on HMIS Talwar shatter armed forces' loyalty to the Raj.
  • 8.
    May 16 & August 16, 1946 (Cabinet Mission Plan & Direct Action Day): Cabinet Mission proposes a 3-tier federation; its breakdown leads to League's Direct Action Day and Great Calcutta Killings.
  • 9.
